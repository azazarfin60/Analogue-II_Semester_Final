# The Barkhausen Criterion
> **Topic:** Oscillators | **Priority:** 🔴 CRITICAL | **Exam Frequency:** 7/8 years

## What is an Oscillator?

An amplifier requires an external input signal to produce an output. An **oscillator** is an amplifier that provides its own input signal. 
It uses a frequency-selective feedback loop to feed a portion of its output back into its input, completely removing the need for any external AC source. 

While negative feedback stabilizes an amplifier, an oscillator intentionally uses **Positive Feedback** to sustain continuous AC waveforms.

---

## 1. The Barkhausen Derivation
*[Appeared in: 2024 Q8(b), 2021 Q4(a), 2019 Q8(b), 2018 Q8(a)]*

This is the most common theoretical question in the Oscillators chapter.

### The System Equation
Imagine an amplifier with a forward gain of $A$ and a feedback network with a transfer fraction of $\beta$. 
Because we are using positive feedback, the signal from the feedback network adds to the input, rather than subtracting.

The closed-loop transfer function ($A_f$) for positive feedback is:
$$
A_f = \frac{V_o}{V_i} = \frac{A}{1 - A\beta}
$$

### The Condition for Oscillation
An oscillator must generate an output voltage ($V_o \neq 0$) even when the external input voltage is absolutely zero ($V_i = 0$).

Look at the equation: $V_o = A_f \times V_i$. 
If $V_i = 0$, the only mathematically possible way for $V_o$ to be non-zero is if $A_f \to \infty$.

For the closed-loop gain $A_f$ to reach infinity, the denominator of the transfer function must be exactly zero:
$$
1 - A\beta = 0
$$
$$
\implies \mathbf{A\beta = 1}
$$

Because $A$ and $\beta$ are complex numbers (they have both magnitude and phase), the equation $A\beta = 1$ contains two strict conditions. These two conditions form the **Barkhausen Criterion**.

---

## The Two Barkhausen Criteria

### 1. The Amplitude (Gain) Condition
The magnitude of the loop gain must be exactly 1.
$$
\boxed{ |A\beta| = 1 }
$$
- If $|A\beta| < 1$, the oscillations will decay and die out.
- If $|A\beta| > 1$, the oscillations will grow exponentially until the amplifier saturates and the sine wave clips into a square wave.

### 2. The Phase Condition
The total phase shift around the entire closed loop must be $360^\circ$ (or $0^\circ$).
$$
\boxed{ \angle A + \angle \beta = 2n\pi } \quad (\text{where } n = 0, 1, 2...)
$$
- The signal travels through the amplifier (which might shift phase) and through the feedback network (which definitely shifts phase). When it arrives back at the summing junction, it must be perfectly in phase with where it started, so it adds constructively.

## 2. Frequency & Amplitude Stability
*[Appeared in: 2022 Q8(a), 2017 Q4(c), 2023 Q4(c)]*

Exams often ask follow-up questions about stability.

### Starting Condition vs. Steady State
If $|A\beta| = 1$ is required for a perfect sine wave, how does the oscillator start in the first place?
1. **Starting:** The circuit is deliberately designed so that the initial loop gain is slightly greater than 1 ($|A\beta| > 1$). When power is turned on, thermal noise triggers the loop, and the oscillations grow rapidly.
2. **Steady State:** As the amplitude grows, the transistors push toward their non-linear saturation regions, which naturally lowers their gain $A$. The amplitude stabilizes precisely at the point where the reduced gain makes $|A\beta| = 1$. 

### Frequency Stability
Why do oscillators drift in frequency over time?
As transistors heat up, their internal parasitic capacitances change. This shifts the phase of the amplifier ($\angle A$). To maintain the Barkhausen phase condition ($360^\circ$), the feedback network ($\angle \beta$) is forced to shift its phase to compensate. Since $\beta$'s phase is tied to frequency, the oscillation frequency drifts!

**How to fix it:** A stable oscillator must have a feedback network with a very steep phase-versus-frequency curve ($d\phi/d\omega$). If the curve is steep, a massive phase shift required to compensate for temperature drift only requires a microscopic change in frequency. **Crystal Oscillators** have the steepest phase curves, making them the most stable oscillators in existence.

## ⚡ Exam Tips
- The mathematical derivation is only 4 lines long! Just write the positive feedback formula $A / (1 - A\beta)$, set the denominator to zero, and break the resulting $A\beta = 1$ into Magnitude and Phase components.
- Do not confuse the Barkhausen condition ($|A\beta| = 1$) with the instability limit in negative feedback systems ($|A\beta| \ge 1$ at $-180^\circ$). They are the same underlying math, just viewed from different perspectives (desired vs undesired).
