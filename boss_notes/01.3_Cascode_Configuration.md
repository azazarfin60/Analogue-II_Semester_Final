# Cascode Configuration
> **Topic:** Multistage Amplifiers | **Priority:** 🟢 OCCASIONAL | **Exam Frequency:** 3/8 years

## What's the Big Idea?

The common emitter (CE) amplifier gives you high voltage gain, but it has a fatal flaw at high frequencies — the **Miller effect**. The collector-base capacitance ($C_{bc}$) gets multiplied by the voltage gain and appears as a massive capacitance at the input, destroying the bandwidth.

The cascode amplifier is an ingenious fix: stack a common-emitter stage on the bottom and a common-base stage on top. The CE stage amplifies the current, while the CB stage passes it to the output with a voltage gain near unity *at the CE stage output*. Since the CE stage's voltage gain is reduced to approximately 1, the Miller multiplication is almost eliminated!

As the "All About Electronics" lecture explains: "The cascode amplifier is the cascade connection of a common emitter amplifier and a common base amplifier. The advantage is that it provides high voltage gain and high output resistance. The output impedance of the cascode stage is $\beta$ times $R_0$."

## The Circuit

```
                  V_CC
                   |
         +----+----+---------------+
         |    |                    |
        [R1]  |                  [R_C]
         |    |                    |
         +----+------- Base(Q2)    |
         |    |                    |
        [R2]  |                Q2 (CB Stage)
         |    |                    |
        [R3]  |         +----------+
         |    |         |   (V_C1 = V_E2)
        ===   |       Q1 (CE Stage)
        GND   |         |
              |        [R_E]
              |         |
             ===       ===
             GND       GND
```

- **Q1 (bottom)** is in Common-Emitter configuration — it amplifies the signal.
- **Q2 (top)** is in Common-Base configuration — it passes the current through while providing high output impedance and eliminating the Miller effect.

---

## DC Bias Analysis

The voltage divider chain $R_1, R_2, R_3$ creates the bias voltages for both transistors:

$$V_{B1} = \frac{R_3}{R_1 + R_2 + R_3} \cdot V_{CC}$$

$$V_{B2} = \frac{R_2 + R_3}{R_1 + R_2 + R_3} \cdot V_{CC}$$

The currents flow in series through both transistors:

$$I_{E1} = \frac{V_{B1} - V_{BE1}}{R_E}$$

$$I_{C2} \approx I_{E2} \approx I_{C1} \approx I_{E1}$$

$$V_{C2} = V_{CC} - I_{C2} R_C$$

The key insight is that **the same current flows through both transistors**. They're stacked in series, so $I_{C1} = I_{E2}$.

---

## Why Does the Miller Effect Disappear?

The Miller effect multiplies the collector-base capacitance by the voltage gain: $C_{Miller} = C_{bc}(1 + |A_v|)$. In a regular CE stage, $|A_v|$ can be 100-300, making $C_{Miller}$ enormous.

In the cascode, the CE stage (Q1) only sees the low impedance of Q2's emitter as its "load." The voltage gain at Q1's collector is approximately:

$$A_{v1} \approx \frac{-g_m \cdot r_{e2}}{1} \approx -1 \quad (\text{if matched transistors})$$

So the Miller capacitance becomes $C_{bc}(1+1) = 2C_{bc}$ — barely anything! The bandwidth is preserved.

Meanwhile, the *overall* voltage gain is still high because Q2 (the CB stage) provides the voltage swing across $R_C$:

$$A_v \approx -g_m \cdot R_C \quad (\text{same as a regular CE stage!})$$

You get the gain of a CE stage with the bandwidth of a CB stage. Best of both worlds.

---

## Key Results

| Parameter | CE Alone | Cascode |
|---|---|---|
| **Voltage Gain** | $-g_m R_C$ | $-g_m R_C$ (same!) |
| **Output Impedance** | $r_o$ | $\beta \cdot r_o$ (much higher!) |
| **Bandwidth** | Limited by Miller | Much wider |
| **Input Impedance** | $\beta r_e$ | $\beta r_e$ (same) |

## ⚡ Exam Tips

- If asked "advantages of cascode," the answer is: (1) eliminates Miller effect, (2) higher bandwidth, (3) higher output impedance, (4) same voltage gain as CE.
- For DC analysis, remember the currents are approximately equal through both transistors: $I_{C1} \approx I_{E2} \approx I_{C2}$.
