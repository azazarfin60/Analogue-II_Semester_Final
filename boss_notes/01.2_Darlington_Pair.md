# Darlington Pair
> **Topic:** Multistage Amplifiers | **Priority:** 🔴 CRITICAL | **Exam Frequency:** 6/8 years

## What is a Darlington Pair and Why Do We Care?

Imagine you need a transistor with a current gain of 10,000. A single BJT gives you $\beta \approx 100-200$. That's nowhere near enough. The Darlington pair solves this by stacking two BJTs together — the emitter of the first drives the base of the second, creating a "super transistor" whose current gain is the *product* of the two individual gains.

As the YouTube lecture (All About Electronics) explains it: "In a Darlington configuration, the emitter current of Q1 becomes the base current of Q2. So whatever current amplification Q1 provides, Q2 amplifies it again."

The practical applications are everywhere — motor drivers, power switching, high-impedance input buffers — anywhere you need massive current amplification with very high input impedance.

---

## The Circuit

```
            Collector (C)
            o-----------+
                        |
                        |
                  C1  |/ Q1
        Base (B) o----|
                      |> E1
                        |
                        +----+
                             |
                       C2  |/ Q2
                           |------> Output Emitter (E)
                           |> E2
```

Both collectors are tied together (connected to the same supply through a load). The emitter of Q1 feeds directly into the base of Q2. The external base current enters at Q1's base, and the output current exits from Q2's emitter.

---

## The Proof: $\beta_D \approx \beta_1 \beta_2$

This derivation appears in nearly every exam. Let's walk through it carefully — the logic is beautifully simple.

**Start with the input.** Let the base current entering Q1 be $I_{B1}$.

**Q1 amplifies it.** The collector current of Q1 is:

$$I_{C1} = \beta_1 I_{B1}$$

**Q1's emitter current** is the sum of its base and collector currents:

$$I_{E1} = I_{C1} + I_{B1} = (\beta_1 + 1) I_{B1}$$

**Here's the key connection:** Q1's emitter current flows directly into Q2's base:

$$I_{B2} = I_{E1} = (\beta_1 + 1) I_{B1}$$

**Q2 amplifies again.** The collector current of Q2 is:

$$I_{C2} = \beta_2 I_{B2} = \beta_2 (\beta_1 + 1) I_{B1}$$

**The total collector current** is the sum from both transistors (both collectors are tied together):

$$I_C = I_{C1} + I_{C2} = \beta_1 I_{B1} + \beta_2(\beta_1 + 1) I_{B1}$$

$$I_C = (\beta_1 + \beta_1\beta_2 + \beta_2) I_{B1}$$

**The overall current gain:**

$$\beta_D = \frac{I_C}{I_{B1}} = \beta_1\beta_2 + \beta_1 + \beta_2$$

**The approximation:** Since $\beta_1$ and $\beta_2$ are both much greater than 1 (typically 50-200), their product $\beta_1\beta_2$ absolutely dominates the sum $\beta_1 + \beta_2$:

$$\boxed{\beta_D \approx \beta_1 \beta_2}$$

For example, if $\beta_1 = \beta_2 = 100$, then $\beta_D \approx 100 \times 100 = 10{,}000$. That's a current gain of ten thousand from just two transistors!

---

## Key Characteristics of the Darlington Pair

| Parameter | Single BJT | Darlington Pair |
|---|---|---|
| **Current Gain** | $\beta$ (100-200) | $\beta_D \approx \beta_1\beta_2$ (10,000+) |
| **Input Impedance** | $\beta r_e$ | $\beta_1 \beta_2 R_E$ (very high) |
| **Voltage Gain** | $-R_C/r_e$ (high) | $\approx 1$ (emitter follower mode) |
| **$V_{BE}$ Drop** | 0.7 V | **1.4 V** (two junctions in series!) |
| **Saturation $V_{CE}$** | ~0.2 V | ~0.9 V (higher than single) |

> **Critical point:** The Darlington has **two** base-emitter junctions in series, so the total $V_{BE}$ drop is $V_{BE1} + V_{BE2} \approx 1.4\text{ V}$, not 0.7 V.

## Worked Example: Darlington Emitter Follower AC Analysis
*[2019 Q2(b)]*

**Given:** $R_B = 2.4\text{ M}\Omega$, $R_E = 510\;\Omega$, $\beta_1 = 50$, $\beta_2 = 120$, $V_{CC} = +16\text{ V}$.

### DC Bias Analysis

$$\beta_D \approx \beta_1 \beta_2 = 50 \times 120 = 6000$$

KVL around the base-emitter loop (note the **two** $V_{BE}$ drops):

$$V_{CC} - I_{B1} R_B - V_{BE1} - V_{BE2} - I_E R_E = 0$$

Substitute $I_E = (\beta_D + 1) I_{B1} \approx 6000 \, I_{B1}$:

$$16 - I_{B1}(2.4\text{M}) - 0.7 - 0.7 - 6000 \, I_{B1}(510) = 0$$

$$14.6 = I_{B1}(2.4\text{M} + 3.06\text{M}) = I_{B1}(5.46\text{M})$$

$$I_{B1} = \frac{14.6}{5.46\text{M}} \approx 2.67\;\mu\text{A}$$

$$I_E = 6000 \times 2.67\;\mu\text{A} \approx 16.02\text{ mA}$$

### Dynamic Resistances

$$r_{e2} = \frac{26\text{ mV}}{16.02\text{ mA}} \approx 1.62\;\Omega$$

The emitter current of Q1 (= base current of Q2):

$$I_{E1} \approx I_{B2} = \frac{I_E}{\beta_2} = \frac{16.02\text{ mA}}{120} \approx 133.5\;\mu\text{A}$$

$$r_{e1} = \frac{26\text{ mV}}{133.5\;\mu\text{A}} \approx 194.7\;\Omega$$

### AC Parameters

**Input impedance at the Darlington base:**

$$Z_{base} \approx \beta_1[\,r_{e1} + \beta_2(r_{e2} + R_E)\,] \approx \beta_1 \beta_2 R_E = 6000 \times 510 \approx 3.06\text{ M}\Omega$$

**Total input impedance:**

$$Z_i = R_B \| Z_{base} = 2.4\text{M} \| 3.06\text{M} \approx 1.34\text{ M}\Omega$$

### Voltage Gain

This is an emitter follower — the gain should be close to 1:

$$A_v = \frac{R_E}{R_E + r_{e2} + r_{e1}/\beta_2} = \frac{510}{510 + 1.62 + 194.7/120} = \frac{510}{513.24} \approx \boxed{0.994}$$

Nearly unity, as expected!

### Current Gain

$$A_i = A_v \times \frac{Z_i}{R_E} = 0.994 \times \frac{1.34\text{M}}{510} \approx \boxed{2612}$$

Massive current amplification with near-unity voltage gain — that's exactly the Darlington's specialty.

## Worked Example: Voltage-Divider Biased Darlington
*[2024 Q2(b)]*

**Given:** $V_{CC} = +27\text{ V}$, $R_{B1} = 470\text{k}$, $R_{B2} = 220\text{k}$, $R_C = 1.2\text{k}$, $R_E = 680\;\Omega$ (bypassed), $\beta_1 = \beta_2 = 110$.

$$\beta_D = 110 \times 110 = 12{,}100$$

**Thevenin at base:**

$$V_{th} = 27 \times \frac{220\text{k}}{470\text{k} + 220\text{k}} = 27 \times 0.319 \approx 8.61\text{ V}$$

$$R_{th} = 470\text{k} \| 220\text{k} = \frac{470 \times 220}{470+220} \approx 149.9\text{ k}\Omega$$

**KVL:**

$$8.61 - I_{B1}(149.9\text{k}) - 1.4 - 12100 \, I_{B1}(680) = 0$$

$$7.21 = I_{B1}(149.9\text{k} + 8.228\text{M}) = I_{B1}(8.378\text{M})$$

$$I_{B1} \approx 0.86\;\mu\text{A}, \quad I_E \approx 10.4\text{ mA}$$

$$r_e = \frac{26}{10.4} \approx 2.5\;\Omega$$

**AC Analysis (with bypassed $R_E$):**

$$Z_{i2\_base} = \beta_1[\,r_{e1} + \beta_2 \cdot r_e\,] \approx \beta_D \cdot r_e = 12100 \times 2.5 = 30.25\text{ k}\Omega$$

$$Z_i = R_{th} \| Z_{i2\_base} = 149.9\text{k} \| 30.25\text{k} \approx 25.2\text{ k}\Omega$$

$$A_v = -\frac{R_C}{r_e} = -\frac{1200}{2.5} = -480$$

$$A_i = A_v \times \frac{Z_i}{R_C} = -480 \times \frac{25.2\text{k}}{1.2\text{k}} \approx -10{,}080$$

## ⚡ Exam Tips & Common Mistakes

1. **Two $V_{BE}$ drops!** The most common mistake is writing $V_{BE} = 0.7\text{ V}$ for the Darlington. It's **1.4 V** (two junctions in series). If you forget this, your entire DC bias will be wrong.
2. **The proof is always asked as theory.** Memorize the 7-line $\beta_D$ derivation — it's worth 3-4 easy marks in nearly every exam.
3. **Emitter follower vs CE mode.** If $R_E$ is NOT bypassed, the Darlington acts as an emitter follower ($A_v \approx 1$, massive $A_i$). If $R_E$ IS bypassed, it acts like a CE amplifier ($A_v = -R_C/r_e$, very high gain).
4. **Don't confuse Feedback Pair with Darlington.** Darlington uses two same-type transistors (NPN-NPN or PNP-PNP). Feedback Pair uses complementary types (NPN-PNP).
