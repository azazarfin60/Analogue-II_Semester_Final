# First-Order Active Filters
> **Topic:** Active Filters | **Priority:** 🟢 OCCASIONAL | **Exam Frequency:** 2/8 years

## 1. First-Order Active HPF Design
*[Appeared in: 2019 Q6(b)]*

A first-order active High-Pass Filter (HPF) is constructed by placing a passive RC high-pass network at the non-inverting input of an op-amp, configured as a non-inverting amplifier.

### The Circuit

```
                    Rf
              +---[  ]---+
              |          |
        Vi o-[C]--(+)--[ Av ]---+---o Vo
                   |
                  [R]
                   |
                  Gnd
```

### The Math
1. **The Cutoff Frequency ($f_c$):** Dictated entirely by the passive RC network at the input.

   $$
   f_c = \frac{1}{2\pi R C}
   $$

2. **The Passband Gain ($A_{CL}$):** Dictated entirely by the feedback resistors of the op-amp.

   $$
   A_{CL} = 1 + \frac{R_f}{R_1}
   $$

### The Drawback & Mitigation
* **The Drawback:** In theory, a high-pass filter should pass all frequencies from $f_c$ up to infinity. In reality, operational amplifiers have a finite **Gain-Bandwidth Product (GBWP)**. At extremely high frequencies, the internal capacitance of the op-amp causes its open-loop gain to drop sharply. Thus, at very high frequencies, the active HPF actually starts rolling off again! It unintentionally transforms into a Bandpass Filter.
* **The Mitigation:** To fix this, you must select high-speed, high-bandwidth operational amplifiers (like BiFET or current-feedback op-amps) to push the unwanted high-frequency roll-off point far beyond your intended operating range.

## 2. Active Bandpass Filter Cascade
*[Appeared in: 2022 Q6(c), 2019 Q6(c)]*

An active Bandpass Filter can be easily constructed by cascading a High-Pass Filter (HPF) and a Low-Pass Filter (LPF) stage in series, provided that the low cutoff frequency ($f_L$) is strictly less than the high cutoff frequency ($f_H$).

```
        Vi o---[ High-Pass Filter (fL) ]---o---[ Low-Pass Filter (fH) ]---o Vo
```
- The HPF stage blocks everything below $f_L$.
- The LPF stage blocks everything above $f_H$.
- The overlapping region between $f_L$ and $f_H$ becomes the Passband.

## 3. Worked Bandpass Design Example
*[Appeared in: 2022 Q6(c)]*

**Problem Details:** Design a wide-bandpass filter with a passband from $50\text{ kHz}$ to $100\text{ kHz}$.

### Step 1: Design the High-Pass Stage
The lower cutoff frequency is $f_L = 50\text{ kHz}$. We will use a standard 1st-order active HPF.
1. Select an arbitrary standard capacitor: **$C_H = 1\text{ nF}$** ($10^{-9}\text{ F}$).
2. Calculate the required resistor $R_H$:

   $$
   R_H = \frac{1}{2\pi f_L C_H} = \frac{1}{2\pi (50 \times 10^3) (1 \times 10^{-9})}
   $$

   $$
   R_H = \frac{1}{2\pi (5 \times 10^{-5})} \approx \mathbf{3.18\text{ k}\Omega}
   $$

### Step 2: Design the Low-Pass Stage
The upper cutoff frequency is $f_H = 100\text{ kHz}$. We will use a standard 1st-order active LPF.
1. Select an arbitrary standard capacitor: **$C_L = 1\text{ nF}$** ($10^{-9}\text{ F}$).
2. Calculate the required resistor $R_L$:

   $$
   R_L = \frac{1}{2\pi f_H C_L} = \frac{1}{2\pi (100 \times 10^3) (1 \times 10^{-9})}
   $$

   $$
   R_L = \frac{1}{2\pi (10^{-4})} \approx \mathbf{1.59\text{ k}\Omega}
   $$

### Final Circuit Configuration
Connect the input signal to the $50\text{kHz}$ HPF stage, and route its output directly into the $100\text{kHz}$ LPF stage. Because active filters have near-zero output impedance, they will not load each other, and the total passband gain will be the product of their individual gains.

## ⚡ Exam Tips
- For the HPF drawback, explicitly write the acronym **"GBWP" (Gain-Bandwidth Product)**. Examiners look for this specific term.
- When asked to "explain the realization of a bandpass filter," always draw the block diagram showing the HPF followed by the LPF. 
- You must state $f_L < f_H$. If you cascade an LPF of 10kHz and an HPF of 50kHz, you don't get a bandpass filter, you get a circuit that blocks absolutely everything!
