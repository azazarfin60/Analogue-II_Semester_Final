# CMRR & Slew Rate
> **Topic:** Op-Amp Fundamentals | **Priority:** 🔴 CRITICAL | **Exam Frequency:** 6/8 years

## 1. Common-Mode Rejection Ratio (CMRR)

An op-amp is designed to amplify the *difference* between its two inputs. But what if a signal arrives at both inputs at the exact same time, with the exact same voltage? This is called a **common-mode signal** ($V_{cm}$). 

In the real world, common-mode signals are usually 50Hz/60Hz electromagnetic noise picked up by long wires acting as antennas. An ideal op-amp would subtract them ($V_{noise} - V_{noise} = 0$) and output nothing. A practical op-amp isn't perfectly balanced, so a tiny bit of that noise leaks through.

**Definition:** CMRR is the ratio of how well the op-amp amplifies the differential signal ($A_d$) compared to how poorly it amplifies the common-mode signal ($A_{cm}$).

$$
\text{CMRR} = \frac{|A_d|}{|A_{cm}|}
$$

It is usually expressed in decibels (dB):

$$
\text{CMRR (dB)} = 20\log_{10} \left( \frac{|A_d|}{|A_{cm}|} \right)
$$

Higher CMRR means better noise rejection. A standard 741 op-amp has a CMRR of $\sim 90\text{ dB}$, meaning it amplifies the difference 31,622 times more than it amplifies the noise.

## 2. Slew Rate (SR)
*[Appeared in: 2019 Q4(b), 2017 Q4(a), 2017 Q6(b)]*

In an ideal world, if you put a square wave into an op-amp, the output instantly snaps to a bigger square wave. In reality, the internal transistor stages have parasitic capacitance. You cannot change the voltage across a capacitor instantly — it takes time to charge up.

**Definition:** Slew Rate is the absolute maximum physical rate of change of the output voltage of an op-amp. It is how fast the output can move from one voltage to another.

$$
SR = \left. \frac{dv_o}{dt} \right|_{max}
$$

It is usually expressed in Volts per microsecond ($\text{V}/\mu\text{s}$). The classic 741 has an SR of $0.5\text{ V}/\mu\text{s}$. 

### The Triangle Wave Distortion Problem
If you input a high-frequency sine wave with a large amplitude, the sine wave asks the output voltage to rise very steeply. If the sine wave asks the output to rise *faster* than the Slew Rate, the op-amp simply can't keep up. The output falls behind, turning the sine wave into a straight-line ramp. 

**Result:** Slew rate limiting distorts high-frequency sine waves into **triangular waves**.

---

## 3. Derivation: Maximum Frequency ($f_{max}$)
How fast can a sine wave be before it gets slew-rate distorted? Let's derive the formula.

**Step 1:** Assume the output is a perfect, undistorted sine wave with peak amplitude $V_p$:

$$
v_o(t) = V_p \sin(2\pi f t)
$$

**Step 2:** Find the required rate of change by taking the derivative:

$$
\frac{dv_o}{dt} = \frac{d}{dt} [V_p \sin(2\pi f t)] = 2\pi f V_p \cos(2\pi f t)
$$

**Step 3:** The maximum rate of change occurs when the cosine term equals 1 (as the wave crosses zero):

$$
\left. \frac{dv_o}{dt} \right|_{max} = 2\pi f V_p
$$

**Step 4:** To prevent distortion, this required rate of change must be less than or equal to the op-amp's physical Slew Rate ($SR$):

$$
SR \ge 2\pi f V_p
$$

**Step 5:** Isolate frequency to find the maximum allowed limit:

$$
\boxed{ f_{max} = \frac{SR}{2\pi V_p} }
$$

## Worked Numerical 1: Find $f_{max}$
*[Appeared in: 2017 Q6(b)]*

**Given:** 
- Inverting op-amp with $R_f = 100\text{ k}\Omega$ and $R_1 = 10\text{ k}\Omega$. 
- Slew rate $SR = 0.5\text{ V}/\mu\text{s}$. 
- Input signal $v_i(t) = 10\sin(2\pi f t)\text{ mV}$ peak. 

**Goal:** Calculate $f_{max}$ without distortion.

**Step 1: Calculate Closed-Loop Gain ($A_{CL}$)**

$$
A_{CL} = -\frac{R_f}{R_1} = -\frac{100\text{ k}}{10\text{ k}} = -10
$$

**Step 2: Determine Output Peak Voltage ($V_p$)**
The input is $10\text{ mV}$ peak. Multiply by the gain magnitude to find the output peak:

$$
V_p = |A_{CL}| \times V_{p(in)} = 10 \times 10\text{ mV} = 100\text{ mV} = \mathbf{0.1\text{ V}}
$$

**Step 3: Calculate $f_{max}$**
First, convert Slew Rate to standard SI units (Volts per second):

$$
SR = 0.5\text{ V}/\mu\text{s} = 0.5 \times 10^6\text{ V/s}
$$

Apply the formula:

$$
f_{max} = \frac{SR}{2\pi V_p} = \frac{0.5 \times 10^6}{2\pi \times 0.1} = \frac{5 \times 10^6}{2\pi} \approx \boxed{ \mathbf{795.8\text{ kHz}} }
$$

## Worked Numerical 2: Find Maximum Input Voltage
*[Appeared in: 2019 Q4(b)]*

**Given:** 
- Closed-loop gain = $10$. 
- Operating frequency $f = 40\text{ kHz}$. 
- Slew rate $SR = 0.5\text{ V}/\mu\text{s}$. 

**Goal:** Find the maximum peak-to-peak input voltage ($V_{p-p(in)}$) before distortion.

**Step 1: Calculate maximum allowed output peak ($V_p$)**
Rearrange the standard formula $SR \ge 2\pi f V_p$:

$$
V_p = \frac{SR}{2\pi f} = \frac{0.5 \times 10^6}{2\pi \times 40,000} = \frac{500,000}{80,000\pi} = \frac{50}{8\pi} \approx \mathbf{1.989\text{ V peak}}
$$

**Step 2: Convert to peak-to-peak output**

$$
V_{p-p(out)} = 2 \times V_p = 2 \times 1.989 = \mathbf{3.978\text{ V}_{p-p}}
$$

**Step 3: Work backwards to find the input**
Divide the output by the amplifier's gain to find what input caused it:

$$
V_{p-p(in)} = \frac{V_{p-p(out)}}{\text{Gain}} = \frac{3.978}{10} \approx \boxed{ \mathbf{0.398\text{ V}_{p-p}} } \quad (\text{or } 398\text{ mV}_{p-p})
$$

## ⚡ Exam Tips
- **Unit Conversion is the #1 Trap:** You MUST convert $\mu\text{s}$ to seconds! $0.5\text{ V}/\mu\text{s}$ is $500,000\text{ V/s}$. If you don't multiply by $10^6$, your frequency answer will be off by a million times.
- The formula uses $V_p$ (peak voltage), NOT RMS voltage and NOT peak-to-peak. If they give you peak-to-peak, divide by 2 before plugging it into the formula.
- The formula cares about the **Output** voltage. If the problem gives you the input voltage, multiply by the gain first.
