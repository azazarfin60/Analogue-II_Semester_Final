# Negative Impedance Converter (NIC)
> **Topic:** Op-Amp Applications | **Priority:** 🟢 OCCASIONAL | **Exam Frequency:** 2/8 years

## What is Negative Impedance?

In a normal (positive) resistor, if you apply a positive voltage, current flows *into* the resistor. The resistor consumes power and dissipates it as heat ($P = I^2 R$). Ohm's law says $V = I \times R$, so $R = V / I$. The ratio of voltage to current is a positive number.

A **Negative Impedance Converter (NIC)** is an active op-amp circuit that simulates a mathematically negative resistor. If you apply a positive voltage to its input terminal, it doesn't draw current in; instead, it pushes current *out* and back into the voltage source!
Instead of consuming power, a negative resistor *supplies* power to the circuit driving it. 

$$
Z_{in} = \frac{V_{in}}{-I_{in}} = -R
$$

This is incredibly useful in oscillator design and to perfectly cancel out unwanted parasitic resistance in transmission lines.

## The NIC Circuit
*[Appeared in: 2023 Q6(b), 2019 Q5(a)]*

The circuit uses both negative feedback (to keep the op-amp stable and linear) and positive feedback (to create the negative resistance effect).

```
                     R2
                +---[  ]---+
                |          |
         V_i o--(+)--[ Av ]-+---o V_o
                |          |
                +---[  ]---+
                     R1
```

- $R_1$ connects from the output back to the **inverting** terminal (-) and then to ground. (Negative feedback divider).
- $R_2$ connects from the output back to the **non-inverting** terminal (+). (Positive feedback).
- The input signal $V_{in}$ is applied to the non-inverting terminal.

## Mathematical Proof of Negative Impedance

This is the standard 5-mark proof asked in exams.

**Goal:** Prove that the impedance looking into the input terminal is mathematically negative.

We use the following labels:
- $R_1$ is the resistor from the inverting terminal (-) to ground.
- $R_f$ is the resistor from the output to the inverting terminal (-).
- $R$ is the positive feedback resistor from the output to the non-inverting terminal (+).
- The input $V_{in}$ is connected to the non-inverting terminal (+).

*(We assume $R_1 = R_f$ for a gain of 2).*

**Step 1: Find the voltage at the inverting terminal**
The voltage at the inverting terminal is set by the voltage divider of $R_1$ and $R_f$:

$$
V_- = V_{out} \left( \frac{R_1}{R_1 + R_f} \right)
$$

If $R_1 = R_f$:

$$
V_- = \frac{V_{out}}{2}
$$

**Step 2: Apply the Virtual Short Principle**
Because there is negative feedback (and we assume it dominates over the positive feedback), the op-amp stays in the linear region and forces the two input pins to be equal:

$$
V_- = V_+
$$

Since the input $V_{in}$ is connected directly to $V_+$:

$$
V_+ = V_{in}
$$

Substitute this into our equation from Step 1:

$$
V_{in} = \frac{V_{out}}{2}
$$

$$
\implies V_{out} = 2 V_{in}
$$

*(The op-amp is acting as a non-inverting amplifier with a gain of +2).*

**Step 3: Calculate the Input Current ($I_{in}$)**
Now, look at the non-inverting terminal where our source is connected. The source $V_{in}$ pushes current through the positive feedback resistor $R$ towards the output $V_{out}$.
By Ohm's law, the current leaving the source and entering the circuit is:

$$
I_{in} = \frac{V_{in} - V_{out}}{R}
$$

**Step 4: Substitute $V_{out}$**
We proved in Step 2 that $V_{out} = 2 V_{in}$. Substitute this into the current equation:

$$
I_{in} = \frac{V_{in} - 2 V_{in}}{R}
$$

$$
I_{in} = \frac{-V_{in}}{R} = -\frac{V_{in}}{R}
$$

**Step 5: Calculate Input Impedance ($Z_{in}$)**
Impedance is defined as voltage divided by current:

$$
Z_{in} = \frac{V_{in}}{I_{in}}
$$

Substitute our expression for $I_{in}$:

$$
Z_{in} = \frac{V_{in}}{-V_{in} / R} = \boxed{ -R }
$$

**Conclusion:** The input impedance is exactly $-R$. The circuit acts as a negative resistor, injecting current back into the input source!

*(Note: If $R_1 \neq R_f$, the general formula is $Z_{in} = -R \frac{R_1}{R_f}$.)*

## ⚡ Exam Tips
- The proof relies entirely on the fact that the op-amp is configured with a non-inverting gain greater than 1 (specifically, a gain of 2). Because $V_{out}$ is larger than $V_{in}$, the current is forced to flow *backwards* through the resistor $R$.
- Don't get confused by the two feedback paths. The negative path ($R_1, R_f$) just sets the voltage gain ($V_{out} = 2V_{in}$). The positive path ($R$) is where you calculate the current.
