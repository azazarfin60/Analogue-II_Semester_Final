# Ideal vs. Practical Op-Amps
> **Topic:** Op-Amp Fundamentals | **Priority:** 🔴 CRITICAL | **Exam Frequency:** 6/8 years

## What is an Op-Amp?

An Operational Amplifier (Op-Amp) is a high-gain, direct-coupled differential amplifier. It takes two inputs (inverting and non-inverting), subtracts them, and multiplies the difference by a massive gain factor. 

In circuit analysis, we almost always assume the op-amp is "ideal" to simplify the math. But in the real world (and in exams), you need to know exactly how a practical op-amp (like the classic $\mu\text{A}741$) falls short of perfection.

## The Comparison Table
*[Appeared in: 2024 Q5(a), 2022 Q5(a), 2017 Q5(a)]*

This table is frequently asked for 3-4 marks. Memorize the ideal values and the physical significance.

| Parameter | Ideal Op-Amp | Practical Op-Amp (e.g. 741) | Physical Significance |
|---|:---:|:---:|---|
| **Open-Loop Gain ($A_{OL}$)** | **$\infty$** | $\sim 2 \times 10^5$ | Amplifies the tiniest difference between inputs into a massive output voltage. |
| **Input Impedance ($Z_i$)** | **$\infty$** | $\sim 2\text{ M}\Omega$ | Draws absolutely *zero* current from the input signal source, preventing loading. |
| **Output Impedance ($Z_o$)** | **$0$** | $\sim 75\ \Omega$ | Acts as a perfect voltage source that can drive any load without the voltage sagging. |
| **Bandwidth ($BW$)** | **$\infty$** | $\sim 1\text{ MHz}$ | Can amplify all signals equally, from DC (0 Hz) up to infinite frequency. |
| **CMRR** | **$\infty$** | $\sim 90\text{ dB}$ | Perfectly rejects noise or interference that appears equally on both input pins. |
| **Slew Rate ($SR$)** | **$\infty$** | $\sim 0.5\text{ V}/\mu\text{s}$ | The output can jump from $-V_{sat}$ to $+V_{sat}$ instantly in 0 seconds. |
| **Input Offset Voltage ($V_{OS}$)** | **$0\text{ V}$** | $\sim 1\text{ mV}$ to $5\text{ mV}$ | If you tie both inputs to 0V ground, the output will be exactly 0V. |

## Understanding the Non-Idealities

Exams sometimes ask you to define these practical imperfections:

### 1. Input Bias Current ($I_B$)
An ideal op-amp draws zero current. A practical op-amp uses BJT transistors at its input. BJTs *must* draw a tiny base current to turn on. 
**Definition:** The average of the DC currents flowing into the inverting ($I_{B-}$) and non-inverting ($I_{B+}$) input terminals required to bias the internal transistors.
$$
I_B = \frac{I_{B+} + I_{B-}}{2}
$$

### 2. Input Offset Current ($I_{OS}$)
If the two internal input BJTs were perfectly identical, their base currents would be equal. In reality, manufacturing isn't perfect.
**Definition:** The absolute difference between the two input bias currents, resulting from slight transistor mismatches.
$$
I_{OS} = |I_{B+} - I_{B-}|
$$

### 3. Input Offset Voltage ($V_{OS}$)
Because of the transistor mismatches mentioned above, if you connect both input pins to exactly $0\text{V}$, the output won't be $0\text{V}$ (it will likely rail to saturation because the tiny mismatch gets amplified by $200,000$).
**Definition:** The DC differential voltage that you must artificially apply across the input terminals to force the output voltage back to exactly $0\text{V}$.

### 4. Power Supply Rejection Ratio (PSRR)
If your $V_{CC}$ battery voltage drops slightly, it shouldn't affect the amplifier. PSRR measures how well the op-amp ignores changes in its power supply.
**Definition:** The ratio of the change in input offset voltage to the corresponding change in power supply voltage.
$$
PSRR = 20\log_{10} \left( \frac{\Delta V_{OS}}{\Delta V_{CC}} \right)
$$
