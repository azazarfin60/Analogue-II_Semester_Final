# RC Phase-Shift Oscillator
> **Topic:** Oscillators | **Priority:** 🟡 IMPORTANT | **Exam Frequency:** 3/8 years

## How It Satisfies Barkhausen

To build an oscillator, we need a total loop phase shift of $360^\circ$ and a loop gain of $1$.

1. **The Amplifier:** We use a standard Inverting Op-Amp. Because it is inverting, it naturally provides a $-180^\circ$ phase shift.
2. **The Feedback Network:** To get the remaining $180^\circ$, we feed the output back through a network of RC high-pass filters. A single RC high-pass filter can theoretically provide up to $90^\circ$ of phase shift, but realistically only about $60^\circ$. Therefore, we must cascade exactly **three** identical RC networks to hit the target $180^\circ$.
   $$ -180^\circ \text{ (Amplifier)} + 180^\circ \text{ (Feedback)} = 0^\circ \text{ (or } 360^\circ \text{)} $$

## 1. Frequency and Gain Derivation
*[Appeared in: 2021 Q4(b), 2018 Q5(c)]*

### The Feedback Circuit
The feedback network consists of three series capacitors ($C_1, C_2, C_3$) and three parallel resistors ($R_1, R_2, R_3$) leading to ground. We assume all Cs are equal and all Rs are equal.

By applying nodal analysis to this 3-stage ladder network, the feedback transfer function $\beta = V_f / V_o$ is derived as:
$$ \beta(j\omega) = \frac{1}{1 - \frac{5}{\omega^2 R^2 C^2} - j \left( \frac{6}{\omega R C} - \frac{1}{\omega^3 R^3 C^3} \right)} $$

### Deriving the Resonant Frequency ($f_o$)
According to Barkhausen, the phase shift must be exactly $180^\circ$. A $180^\circ$ phase shift means the signal is purely real (and negative). There can be no imaginary component.
Therefore, we must set the imaginary part of the denominator to zero:
$$ \frac{6}{\omega RC} - \frac{1}{\omega^3 R^3 C^3} = 0 $$

Move the negative term over:
$$ \frac{6}{\omega RC} = \frac{1}{\omega^3 R^3 C^3} $$

Multiply both sides by $\omega^3 R^3 C^3$:
$$ 6 \omega^2 R^2 C^2 = 1 $$

Solve for $\omega$:
$$ \omega^2 = \frac{1}{6 R^2 C^2} \implies \omega = \frac{1}{\sqrt{6} RC} $$

Since $\omega = 2\pi f_o$, we get the final frequency formula:
$$ \boxed{ f_o = \frac{1}{2\pi \sqrt{6} RC} } $$

### Deriving the Gain Requirement ($|A| \ge 29$)
Now that we know the frequency where the phase shift is perfect, how much does the RC network attenuate the signal? Let's plug $\omega^2 R^2 C^2 = 1/6$ back into the real part of the transfer function.

$$ \beta = \frac{1}{1 - 5 \left( \frac{1}{\omega^2 R^2 C^2} \right)} $$
$$ \beta = \frac{1}{1 - 5(6)} = \frac{1}{1 - 30} = -\frac{1}{29} $$
*(The negative sign confirms the $180^\circ$ phase shift).*

The Barkhausen amplitude condition states $|A\beta| \ge 1$.
$$ |A \left( -\frac{1}{29} \right)| \ge 1 \implies \boxed{ |A| \ge 29 } $$

**Conclusion:** To sustain oscillations, the inverting amplifier must be designed with a voltage gain of at least 29!

## 2. Worked Design Problem
*[Appeared in: 2019 Q8(c)]*

**Problem Details:**
Design an op-amp based 3-stage RC phase-shift oscillator to operate at a resonant frequency of $f_o = 400\text{ Hz}$.

### Step 1: Select a Practical Capacitor
We must pick an arbitrary, standard capacitor value to start.
Let **$C = 0.1\ \mu\text{F}$** ($10^{-7}\text{ F}$).

### Step 2: Calculate the Required Feedback Resistor ($R$)
Using the derived frequency formula:
$$ f_o = \frac{1}{2\pi \sqrt{6} RC} $$
Rearrange to solve for $R$:
$$ R = \frac{1}{2\pi \sqrt{6} f_o C} $$
$$ R = \frac{1}{2\pi \times 2.449 \times 400 \times 10^{-7}} = \frac{1}{0.0006155} \approx \mathbf{1624.7\ \Omega} $$
We will select a standard resistor value of **$1.62\text{ k}\Omega$** for the three feedback resistors.

### Step 3: Design the Inverting Amplifier
The op-amp is configured as a standard inverting amplifier with an input resistor $R_1$ and a feedback resistor $R_F$. 
To prevent loading effects from disrupting the delicate RC math, the input resistor $R_1$ is usually chosen to match the network resistor $R$.
$$ R_1 = 1.62\text{ k}\Omega $$

We proved the gain must be at least 29 ($|A| \ge 29$).
For an inverting amplifier, $|A| = R_F / R_1$.
$$ \frac{R_F}{1.62\text{ k}\Omega} \ge 29 \implies R_F \ge 29 \times 1.62\text{ k}\Omega \approx \mathbf{46.98\text{ k}\Omega} $$
We will select a standard resistor value of **$47\text{ k}\Omega$** for $R_F$ to guarantee oscillations start.

## ⚡ Exam Tips
- The derivation from the raw $\beta$ formula to $f_o$ and $|A|=29$ is heavily tested. Memorize setting the imaginary part to zero to find $\omega$, then plugging that $\omega$ into the real part to find $\beta = 1/29$.
- In design questions, always start by arbitrarily selecting a capacitor in the nanoFarad or microFarad range ($10\text{nF}$ or $0.1\mu\text{F}$ are safe bets). Do not pick $1\text{ Farad}$.
