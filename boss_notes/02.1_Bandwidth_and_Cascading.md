# Bandwidth and Cascading Effect
> **Topic:** Frequency Response | **Priority:** 🟡 IMPORTANT | **Exam Frequency:** 4/8 years

## Why Cascading Shrinks Bandwidth

When we cascade amplifier stages (connecting the output of one to the input of the next), we multiply their voltage gains. This is great for making signals larger! But there is a hidden cost: **every stage you add shrinks the overall bandwidth.**

Why does this happen?
1. **At Low Frequencies:** The coupling and bypass capacitors act as high-pass filters. When you cascade stages, you are chaining multiple high-pass filters together. The overall signal gets attenuated more heavily at low frequencies. As a result, the **lower cutoff frequency ($f_L$) shifts higher**.
2. **At High Frequencies:** The internal parasitic junction capacitances of the transistors act as low-pass filters. Chaining stages means chaining low-pass filters. The signal gets attenuated more heavily at high frequencies. Therefore, the **upper cutoff frequency ($f_H$) shifts lower**.

Since Bandwidth ($BW$) is the distance between the cutoffs ($BW = f_H - f_L$), squeezing both ends inward severely reduces the usable bandwidth.

---

## The Mathematical Derivation
*[Appeared in: 2023 Q3(b)]*

Let's mathematically prove exactly how much the upper cutoff frequency shrinks when we cascade $n$ identical amplifier stages. 

### Step 1: Single Stage High-Frequency Response
For a single stage, the voltage gain at high frequencies falls off due to parasitic capacitance. The relative gain equation is:

$$
\frac{A_v(f)}{A_{mid}} = \frac{1}{1 + j(f/f_H)}
$$

Where $A_{mid}$ is the mid-band gain, and $f_H$ is the single-stage upper cutoff frequency.

### Step 2: Cascaded Response
If we cascade $n$ identical, non-interacting stages, their overall gain is the product of their individual gains. The magnitude of the relative gain becomes:

$$
\left| \frac{A_{v(overall)}}{A_{mid(overall)}} \right| = \left( \frac{1}{\sqrt{1 + (f/f_H)^2}} \right)^n
$$

### Step 3: Finding the Overall Cutoff Frequency
By definition, the overall upper cutoff frequency ($f_{H(overall)}$) occurs when the overall relative gain magnitude drops to $1/\sqrt{2}$ (or -3dB, or 0.707). Let's substitute $1/\sqrt{2}$ for the gain and $f_{H(overall)}$ for the frequency:

$$
\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}} = \left( \frac{1}{\sqrt{1 + (f_{H(overall)}/f_H)^2}} \right)^n
$$

Square both sides to get rid of the square roots:

$$
\frac{1}{2} = \left( \frac{1}{1 + (f_{H(overall)}/f_H)^2} \right)^n
$$

Invert both sides:

$$
2 = \left[ 1 + \left( \frac{f_{H(overall)}}{f_H} \right)^2 \right]^n
$$

Take the $n$-th root of both sides:

$$
2^{1/n} = 1 + \left( \frac{f_{H(overall)}}{f_H} \right)^2
$$

Isolate the frequency ratio:

$$
\left( \frac{f_{H(overall)}}{f_H} \right)^2 = 2^{1/n} - 1
$$

Take the square root to get the final formula:

$$
\boxed{ f_{H(overall)} = f_H \sqrt{2^{1/n} - 1} }
$$

### The Lower Cutoff Formula
By following the exact same mathematical process using the low-frequency single-stage equation, we get the formula for the overall lower cutoff frequency:

$$
\boxed{ f_{L(overall)} = \frac{f_L}{\sqrt{2^{1/n} - 1}} }
$$

---

## What Does This Mean in Practice?

Let's plug in $n=2$ (a two-stage amplifier) into the high-frequency formula:

$$
f_{H(overall)} = f_H \sqrt{2^{1/2} - 1} = f_H \sqrt{\sqrt{2} - 1} = f_H \sqrt{1.414 - 1} = f_H \sqrt{0.414} \approx 0.643 f_H
$$

Just by adding a second identical stage, the upper cutoff frequency dropped to **~64%** of its original value! The more stages you add, the worse it gets. This proves that you trade bandwidth for gain when cascading amplifiers.
