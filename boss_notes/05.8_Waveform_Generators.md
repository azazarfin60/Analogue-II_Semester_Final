# Non-Linear Waveform Generators
> **Topic:** Op-Amp Applications | **Priority:** 🟢 OCCASIONAL | **Exam Frequency:** 1/8 years

## Why Non-Linear?

Most op-amp circuits we study (like amplifiers, integrators, and summers) use **negative feedback**. Negative feedback keeps the op-amp in its linear region, where the output is directly proportional to the input.

Waveform generators (oscillators) use **positive feedback**. Positive feedback deliberately drives the op-amp into instability, forcing the output to instantly snap back and forth between positive saturation ($+V_{sat}$) and negative saturation ($-V_{sat}$). Because the output is always saturating, the op-amp is operating in its non-linear region.

---

## 1. Pulse Generator (Astable Multivibrator)
*[Appeared in: 2017 Q6(c)]*

A standard astable multivibrator generates a symmetrical square wave (50% duty cycle). A **Pulse Generator** is simply a modified astable multivibrator that generates an *asymmetrical* square wave (e.g., 10% ON, 90% OFF), creating a train of narrow pulses.

### How it works:
1. **The Core Circuit:** It is a Schmitt Trigger (positive feedback creating upper and lower thresholds) with an RC circuit connected to the inverting terminal (negative feedback).
2. **Symmetrical Operation:** The capacitor $C$ charges up through a feedback resistor $R$ until it hits the Upper Trigger Point ($V_{UT}$). The op-amp output snaps to $-V_{sat}$. The capacitor then discharges through the *same* resistor $R$ until it hits the Lower Trigger Point ($V_{LT}$). The output snaps back to $+V_{sat}$. Since charge time equals discharge time, you get a 50% square wave.
3. **The Pulse Modification:** To make a pulse generator, we place a diode $D$ in parallel with a portion of the feedback resistor network.
   - **Charging:** When the output is $+V_{sat}$, the diode is forward-biased. It creates a short-circuit bypass around the resistor. The capacitor charges *extremely rapidly* through this low-resistance path.
   - **Discharging:** When the output is $-V_{sat}$, the diode is reverse-biased. It acts as an open circuit. The capacitor is forced to discharge *slowly* through the full high-resistance path.
4. **The Result:** The op-amp spends very little time at $+V_{sat}$ (fast charge) and a long time at $-V_{sat}$ (slow discharge). This produces a train of narrow positive pulses.

## 2. Triangular Wave Generator
*[Appeared in: 2017 Q6(c)]*

A triangular wave generator is created by cascading two different op-amp circuits together in a loop:
1. **A Schmitt Trigger** (Astable Multivibrator)
2. **A Practical Integrator**

### How it works:
1. The **Schmitt Trigger** is designed to output a square wave ($\pm V_{sat}$).
2. This square wave is fed directly into the input of the **Integrator**.
3. We know from calculus that the integral of a constant is a linear ramp ($y = mx$). 
   - When the square wave is at $+V_{sat}$, the integrator integrates a positive constant, producing a steady, linear **downward ramp** (because the integrator is an *inverting* integrator).
   - When the square wave is at $-V_{sat}$, the integrator integrates a negative constant, producing a steady, linear **upward ramp**.
4. The output of the integrator is fed *back* into the input of the Schmitt trigger to trigger the state changes.
5. **The Result:** The constant integration of alternating positive and negative constants produces a highly symmetrical, perfectly straight triangular waveform.

*(Note: The frequency and amplitude of the triangular wave are controlled by the RC time constant of the integrator and the threshold voltages of the Schmitt trigger).*
