# LC Oscillators (Colpitts, Hartley, Clapp)
> **Topic:** Oscillators | **Priority:** 🟡 IMPORTANT | **Exam Frequency:** 4/8 years

## Why Use LC Oscillators?
RC oscillators (Phase-Shift, Wien Bridge) are excellent for low frequencies (audio range, up to $100\text{ kHz}$). But at radio frequencies (RF, $>1\text{ MHz}$), the phase shift of the op-amp itself ruins the math, and the required RC values become impractically small.

For high frequencies, we use **LC Tank Oscillators**. An inductor ($L$) and capacitor ($C$) connected in parallel naturally trade energy back and forth, resonating at a very specific high frequency. We place this LC tank in the feedback path of a high-speed transistor (BJT or JFET).

---

## 1. Colpitts Oscillator Derivation
*[Appeared in: 2021 Q4(a), 2019 Q6(a), 2018 Q5(b), CT4 Q2]*

The Colpitts oscillator is identifiable by its "split-capacitor" tank. It uses one inductor ($L$) and two capacitors ($C_1, C_2$). The feedback is tapped from the junction between the two capacitors.

### The Feedback Tank Circuit
```
                         L
               +-------[   ]-------+
               |                   |
        Vo o---+----[ C1 ]----+----+---o Vf
                              |
                             Gnd
```

### Proof of Resonant Frequency ($f_o$)
For sustained oscillations to exist, the Barkhausen criterion demands a purely real feedback path. This means the sum of the reactances around the closed LC tank loop must perfectly cancel out (sum to zero) at the resonant frequency:

$$
X_L + X_{C1} + X_{C2} = 0
$$

Substitute the standard dynamic reactive impedance formulas:

$$
j\omega L + \frac{1}{j\omega C_1} + \frac{1}{j\omega C_2} = 0
$$

In complex math, $1/j$ is exactly equal to $-j$. Let's substitute that:

$$
j\omega L - j \frac{1}{\omega C_1} - j \frac{1}{\omega C_2} = 0
$$

Divide the entire equation by $j$:

$$
\omega L - \frac{1}{\omega C_1} - \frac{1}{\omega C_2} = 0
$$

Move the capacitor terms to the other side:

$$
\omega L = \frac{1}{\omega C_1} + \frac{1}{\omega C_2}
$$

Factor out $1/\omega$:

$$
\omega L = \frac{1}{\omega} \left( \frac{1}{C_1} + \frac{1}{C_2} \right)
$$

Multiply both sides by $\omega$ to isolate it:

$$
\omega^2 L = \frac{C_1 + C_2}{C_1 C_2}
$$

$$
\omega^2 = \frac{1}{L} \left( \frac{C_1 + C_2}{C_1 C_2} \right)
$$

Notice that $\left(\frac{C_1 C_2}{C_1 + C_2}\right)$ is the formula for two capacitors in series. Let's call this equivalent capacitance **$C_{eq}$**:

$$
\omega^2 = \frac{1}{L C_{eq}} \implies \omega = \frac{1}{\sqrt{L C_{eq}}}
$$

Since $\omega = 2\pi f_o$, we get the final resonant frequency:

$$
\boxed{ f_o = \frac{1}{2\pi \sqrt{L \left( \frac{C_1 C_2}{C_1 + C_2} \right)}} }
$$

## 2. Hartley & Clapp Oscillators

- **Hartley Oscillator:** The exact inverse of the Colpitts. It uses a "split-inductor" tank (two inductors $L_1, L_2$ and one capacitor $C$).

$$
f_o = \frac{1}{2\pi \sqrt{C (L_1 + L_2)}}
$$

- **Clapp Oscillator:** An improved version of the Colpitts. It adds a third capacitor ($C_3$) in series with the inductor. This isolates the tank from the transistor's parasitic capacitances, massively improving frequency stability. 

### Worked Clapp JFET Oscillator Numerical
*[Appeared in: 2017 Q4(b)]*

**Problem Details:**
A Clapp JFET oscillator has series tank inductors $L_1 = 30\ \mu\text{H}$ and $L_2 = 10\ \mu\text{H}$ in series, and split capacitors $C_1 = 0.01\ \mu\text{F}$ and $C_2 = 0.01\ \mu\text{F}$ in series. 
Calculate (i) resonant frequency $f_o$, (ii) feedback factor $\beta$, and (iii) minimum required voltage gain $A_v$.

**Step 1: Calculate Equivalent Inductance ($L_{eq}$)**
Inductors in series add directly:

$$
L_{eq} = L_1 + L_2 = 30\ \mu\text{H} + 10\ \mu\text{H} = \mathbf{40\ \mu\text{H}}
$$

**Step 2: Calculate Equivalent Capacitance ($C_{eq}$)**
Capacitors in series add inversely:

$$
C_{eq} = \frac{C_1 C_2}{C_1 + C_2} = \frac{0.01 \times 0.01}{0.01 + 0.01} = \mathbf{0.005\ \mu\text{F}} \quad (5\text{ nF})
$$

**Step 3: Calculate Resonant Frequency ($f_o$)**

$$
f_o = \frac{1}{2\pi \sqrt{L_{eq} C_{eq}}} = \frac{1}{2\pi \sqrt{(40 \times 10^{-6}) \times (5 \times 10^{-9})}}
$$

$$
f_o = \frac{1}{2\pi \sqrt{2 \times 10^{-13}}} = \frac{1}{2\pi \times (4.472 \times 10^{-7})} \approx \boxed{ \mathbf{355.9\text{ kHz}} }
$$

**Step 4: Calculate Feedback Factor ($\beta$)**
For split-capacitor tanks, the feedback fraction is determined by the capacitor ratio:

$$
\beta = \frac{C_1}{C_2} = \frac{0.01\ \mu\text{F}}{0.01\ \mu\text{F}} = \boxed{ \mathbf{1} }
$$

**Step 5: Determine Minimum Voltage Gain ($A_v$)**
By the Barkhausen criterion, $|A_v \beta| \ge 1$:

$$
A_v \ge \frac{1}{\beta} \implies \boxed{ A_v \ge 1 }
$$

## 3. Crystal Oscillators
*[Appeared in: 2020 Q5(d)]*

Crystal oscillators replace the LC tank with a slice of quartz crystal. Due to the **Piezoelectric Effect**, the crystal vibrates mechanically when an AC voltage is applied, acting exactly like an LC tank but with a Quality Factor ($Q$) of $100,000$ (compared to $100$ for standard LC tanks). 
This enormous $Q$-factor makes them practically immune to temperature drift, giving them the highest frequency stability of any standard oscillator.

## ⚡ Exam Tips
- For the Colpitts derivation, remembering the starting line ($X_L + X_{C1} + X_{C2} = 0$) guarantees you the marks, as the rest is just basic algebra.
- Pay attention to unit prefixes in numericals! $\mu\text{H}$ is $10^{-6}$ and $\mu\text{F}$ is $10^{-6}$. If you get the powers of ten wrong under the square root, your answer will be wildly incorrect.
