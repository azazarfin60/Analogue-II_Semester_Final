# Log and Antilog Amplifiers
> **Topic:** Op-Amp Applications | **Priority:** 🟢 OCCASIONAL | **Exam Frequency:** 2/8 years

## Why Use Logarithmic Amplifiers?

In signal processing, we often deal with signals that have a massive dynamic range — for example, a radio receiver might need to process a $1\text{ }\mu\text{V}$ signal one second, and a $1\text{ V}$ signal the next. A linear amplifier would either fail to amplify the small signal or completely saturate on the large signal. 

A Logarithmic Amplifier solves this by compressing the dynamic range. It provides huge gain for tiny signals and very small gain for massive signals, keeping everything within a usable voltage range.

How do we build one? We exploit the fact that the PN junction in a diode or BJT has an inherently logarithmic relationship between voltage and current.

## 1. The Logarithmic Amplifier Derivation
*[Appeared in: 2022 Q7(c), 2020 Q7(b)]*

The classic log amp places a BJT inside the feedback loop of an inverting amplifier.

### The Circuit

```
                     Q1 (BJT)
                   +--|<|---+ (Collector to Emitter)
                   |  C   E |
        Vin o-[ R ]+--o (-) |
                      |     |
        Gnd o--------(+)----+---o Vout
```
- The input $V_{in}$ is applied through resistor $R$ to the inverting terminal.
- The BJT has its Base grounded, its Collector at the inverting terminal, and its Emitter at the output.

### The Step-by-Step Derivation

**Step 1: Apply Virtual Ground**
Because the non-inverting terminal is grounded, the inverting terminal is a virtual ground ($V_- = 0\text{V}$).

**Step 2: Find the Input Current**
The current flowing from the source through resistor $R$ is:

$$
I_{in} = \frac{V_{in} - 0}{R} = \frac{V_{in}}{R}
$$

**Step 3: Track the Current**
Since an ideal op-amp draws zero input current, all of $I_{in}$ must flow up and into the Collector of the BJT.

$$
I_C = I_{in} = \frac{V_{in}}{R}
$$

**Step 4: The BJT Current Equation**
We know from semiconductor physics that the collector current of a BJT is exponentially related to its Base-Emitter voltage ($V_{BE}$):

$$
I_C = I_s e^{V_{BE} / V_T}
$$

*(Where $I_s$ is the reverse saturation current, and $V_T$ is the thermal voltage $\approx 26\text{ mV}$).*

**Step 5: Define $V_{BE}$ in terms of the circuit**
The base of the BJT is tied to the virtual ground ($V_B = 0$). The emitter is tied to the output ($V_E = V_{out}$).
Therefore:

$$
V_{BE} = V_B - V_E = 0 - V_{out} = -V_{out}
$$

**Step 6: Substitute and Solve**
Plug $-V_{out}$ into the exponent, and substitute $I_C$ with $V_{in}/R$:

$$
\frac{V_{in}}{R} = I_s e^{-V_{out} / V_T}
$$

Divide by $I_s$:

$$
\frac{V_{in}}{I_s R} = e^{-V_{out} / V_T}
$$

Take the natural logarithm ($\ln$) of both sides to cancel the exponential:

$$
\ln \left( \frac{V_{in}}{I_s R} \right) = -\frac{V_{out}}{V_T}
$$

Rearrange to solve for $V_{out}$:

$$
\boxed{ V_{out} = -V_T \ln \left( \frac{V_{in}}{I_s R} \right) }
$$

**Result:** The output voltage is proportional to the natural logarithm of the input voltage!

## 2. The Antilogarithmic Amplifier

If we swap the positions of the BJT and the resistor, we reverse the mathematical operation, creating an Antilog (Exponential) amplifier.

### The Circuit
- The BJT is placed at the input (Emitter to source, Base grounded, Collector to inverting terminal).
- The resistor $R$ is placed in the feedback loop.

### The Math
1. The input voltage is applied directly across the Base-Emitter junction: $V_{BE} = V_{in}$.
2. This generates an exponential collector current: $I_C = I_s e^{V_{in} / V_T}$.
3. This entire current is forced to flow through the feedback resistor $R$.
4. The output voltage is just the voltage drop across $R$:

   $$
   V_{out} = -I_C R
   $$

   $$
   \boxed{ V_{out} = -R I_s e^{V_{in} / V_T} }
   $$

**Result:** The output voltage is exponentially proportional to the input voltage.

## ⚡ Exam Tips
- For the log amp, **make sure you draw the BJT in the correct orientation**. The Collector must connect to the virtual ground (inverting pin) and the Emitter must connect to the output. If you draw it backwards, the derivation fails.
- Do not forget the negative sign on $V_{out}$ when substituting $V_{BE} = -V_{out}$. This is the most common mathematical mistake.
- State that $V_T$ is the thermal voltage ($\approx 26\text{mV}$ at room temperature).
