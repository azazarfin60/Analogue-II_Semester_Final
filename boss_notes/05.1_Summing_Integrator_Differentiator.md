# Summing, Integrator, and Differentiator
> **Topic:** Op-Amp Applications | **Priority:** 🔴 CRITICAL | **Exam Frequency:** 7/8 years

## 1. The Inverting Summing Amplifier

The summing amplifier is the backbone of analog computing. It allows us to add multiple voltage signals together, each with its own independent scaling factor (gain).

### The Circuit
```
        V1 o---[ R1 ]---+
                        |      Rf
        V2 o---[ R2 ]---+---[    ]---+
                        |            |
        V3 o---[ R3 ]---+--(-)       |
                           [ Av ]----+---o Vout
        Gnd o--------------(+)
```

### The Math
Because the non-inverting terminal is grounded, the inverting terminal becomes a **Virtual Ground** ($0\text{V}$). 
This means the current from each input branch is completely independent of the others. 
$$
I_1 = \frac{V_1}{R_1}, \quad I_2 = \frac{V_2}{R_2}, \quad I_3 = \frac{V_3}{R_3}
$$

By KCL, all these currents must sum together and flow through the feedback resistor $R_f$:
$$
I_{total} = I_1 + I_2 + I_3
$$

The output voltage is simply the voltage drop across $R_f$ (but negative, because current flows *towards* the output):
$$
V_{out} = -I_{total} R_f = - \left( \frac{V_1}{R_1} + \frac{V_2}{R_2} + \frac{V_3}{R_3} \right) R_f
$$

$$
\boxed{ V_{out} = - \left( \frac{R_f}{R_1}V_1 + \frac{R_f}{R_2}V_2 + \frac{R_f}{R_3}V_3 \right) }
$$

## 2. Practical Integrators & Differentiators
*[Appeared in: 2024 Q5(b), 2023 Q5(b)]*

In theory, making an integrator or differentiator is simple: just replace either $R_1$ or $R_f$ with a capacitor. 
- **Ideal Integrator:** Input resistor $R$, feedback capacitor $C$.
- **Ideal Differentiator:** Input capacitor $C$, feedback resistor $R$.

But ideal circuits fail in the real world. Exams frequently ask *why* they fail and *how* to fix them.

### The Practical Integrator Problem & Solution
**The Problem:** The feedback element is a capacitor. At DC ($0\text{ Hz}$), a capacitor acts like an open circuit (infinite resistance). The DC gain of the op-amp becomes $A_v = -R_f/R_{in} = -\infty/R = -\infty$. Even a microscopic $1\text{ mV}$ DC offset voltage at the input will be integrated infinitely over time, causing the output voltage to slowly ramp up until it hits the power supply rail ($V_{sat}$) and crashes the amplifier.

**The Solution:** Connect a large physical resistor $R_f$ in parallel with the feedback capacitor $C$. 
This provides a DC path for the current, capping the maximum low-frequency gain at a safe value ($-R_f/R_{in}$) and preventing the op-amp from saturating.

### The Practical Differentiator Problem & Solution
**The Problem:** The gain of an ideal differentiator increases linearly with frequency ($|A_v| = \omega RC$). At very high frequencies, the gain becomes massive. This means the circuit will aggressively amplify any high-frequency electrical noise (hiss) present in the room, completely drowning out your actual low-frequency signal. Furthermore, this high-frequency amplification often pushes the op-amp into instability and oscillation.

**The Solution:** Add a small resistor $R_i$ in series with the input capacitor, and a small capacitor $C_f$ in parallel with the feedback resistor. 
This turns the circuit into a low-pass filter at very high frequencies, rolling off the gain before the noise can be amplified to dangerous levels.
