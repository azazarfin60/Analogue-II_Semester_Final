# First-Order Active Filters
> **Topic:** Active Filters | **Priority:** 🟢 OCCASIONAL | **Exam Frequency:** 2/8 years

## Basic Knowledge: Order and Cutoff

A filter does not instantly block frequencies. It gradually fades them out. The **Cutoff Frequency ($f_c$)** is the exact point where the signal power drops by half.

The **Order** of a filter tells you how fast the filter fades out the unwanted frequencies past the cutoff point. 
A first-order filter uses exactly one reactive component (like a single capacitor). It has a gentle fade-out slope of -20 dB per decade. This means if you increase the frequency by ten times, the signal strength drops by 20 decibels. 
A second-order filter uses two reactive components. It fades out twice as fast (-40 dB per decade).

First-order active filters are incredibly simple. You just take a basic passive resistor-capacitor (RC) filter and connect it directly to the input of an operational amplifier.

---

## 1. First-Order Active HPF Design
*[Appeared in: 2019 Q6(b)]*

A first-order active High-Pass Filter (HPF) is constructed by placing a passive RC high-pass network at the non-inverting input of an op-amp. The op-amp is configured as a standard non-inverting amplifier.

### The Circuit Diagram

```
                    Rf
              +---[  ]---+
              |          |
        Vi o-[C]--(+)--[ Av ]---+---o Vo
                   |
                  [R]
                   |
                  Gnd
```

### The Math
1. **The Cutoff Frequency ($f_c$):** This is dictated entirely by the passive RC network at the input.

$$
f_c = \frac{1}{2\pi R C}
$$

2. **The Passband Gain ($A_{CL}$):** This is dictated entirely by the feedback resistors of the op-amp.

$$
A_{CL} = 1 + \frac{R_f}{R_1}
$$

### The Drawback and Mitigation
* **The Drawback:** In theory, a perfect high-pass filter should pass all frequencies from the cutoff $f_c$ all the way up to infinity. In reality, operational amplifiers have a finite **Gain-Bandwidth Product (GBWP)** limit. At extremely high frequencies, the internal capacitance of the op-amp causes its open-loop gain to drop sharply. Because of this physical limit, at very high frequencies, the active HPF actually starts blocking signals again! It unintentionally transforms into a Bandpass Filter.
* **The Mitigation:** To fix this problem, you must select high-speed, high-bandwidth operational amplifiers. Specialized chips like BiFET or current-feedback op-amps push this unwanted high-frequency limit far beyond your intended operating range.

## 2. Active Bandpass Filter Cascade
*[Appeared in: 2022 Q6(c), 2019 Q6(c)]*

An active Bandpass Filter can be easily constructed by cascading a High-Pass Filter (HPF) and a Low-Pass Filter (LPF) stage in series. You must ensure that the low cutoff frequency ($f_L$) is strictly less than the high cutoff frequency ($f_H$).

```
        Vi o---[ High-Pass Filter (fL) ]---o---[ Low-Pass Filter (fH) ]---o Vo
```
- The HPF stage blocks everything below $f_L$.
- The LPF stage blocks everything above $f_H$.
- The overlapping region directly between $f_L$ and $f_H$ becomes the usable Passband.

## 3. Worked Bandpass Design Example
*[Appeared in: 2022 Q6(c)]*

**Problem Details:** Design a wide-bandpass filter with a passband from $50\text{ kHz}$ to $100\text{ kHz}$.

### Step 1: Design the High-Pass Stage
The lower cutoff frequency is $f_L = 50\text{ kHz}$. We will use a standard 1st-order active HPF.
1. Select an arbitrary standard capacitor: **$C_H = 1\text{ nF}$** ($10^{-9}\text{ F}$).
2. Calculate the required resistor $R_H$:

$$
R_H = \frac{1}{2\pi f_L C_H} = \frac{1}{2\pi (50 \times 10^3) (1 \times 10^{-9})}
$$

$$
R_H = \frac{1}{2\pi (5 \times 10^{-5})} \approx \mathbf{3.18\text{ k}\Omega}
$$

### Step 2: Design the Low-Pass Stage
The upper cutoff frequency is $f_H = 100\text{ kHz}$. We will use a standard 1st-order active LPF.
1. Select an arbitrary standard capacitor: **$C_L = 1\text{ nF}$** ($10^{-9}\text{ F}$).
2. Calculate the required resistor $R_L$:

$$
R_L = \frac{1}{2\pi f_H C_L} = \frac{1}{2\pi (100 \times 10^3) (1 \times 10^{-9})}
$$

$$
R_L = \frac{1}{2\pi (10^{-4})} \approx \mathbf{1.59\text{ k}\Omega}
$$

### Final Circuit Configuration
Connect the input signal directly to the $50\text{kHz}$ HPF stage. Route its output directly into the $100\text{kHz}$ LPF stage. Because active filters have near-zero output impedance, they will not load each other. The total passband gain will be the exact mathematical product of their individual gains.

## ⚡ Exam Tips
- **The GBWP Keyword.** For the HPF drawback, you must explicitly write the acronym **"GBWP" (Gain-Bandwidth Product)**. Examiners look for this specific term.
- **Always draw the blocks.** When asked to "explain the realization of a bandpass filter," always draw the block diagram showing the HPF block followed by the LPF block. 
- **Check your logic.** You must state $f_L < f_H$. If you cascade an LPF of 10kHz and an HPF of 50kHz, you do not get a bandpass filter. You get a completely useless circuit that blocks absolutely everything!
