# The Barkhausen Criterion
> **Topic:** Oscillators | **Priority:** 🔴 CRITICAL | **Exam Frequency:** 7/8 years

## Basic Knowledge: The Infinite Loop

An amplifier requires an external input signal to produce an output. If you unplug your guitar, the guitar amplifier goes silent. 

An **oscillator** is different. It is an amplifier that provides its own input signal. It does not need an external source. It generates a continuous alternating current (AC) waveform completely on its own.

How does it do this? It uses a feedback loop. It takes a portion of its output signal and feeds it right back into its input. 

Normally, amplifiers use negative feedback. Negative feedback subtracts from the input to keep the amplifier stable and quiet. An oscillator intentionally uses **Positive Feedback**. The returned signal adds to the input. This creates a continuous, self-sustaining loop. The signal goes round and round, generating a permanent sine wave.

---

## 1. The Barkhausen Derivation
*[Appeared in: 2024 Q8(b), 2021 Q4(a), 2019 Q8(b), 2018 Q8(a)]*

This is the most common theoretical question in the Oscillators chapter.

### The System Equation
Imagine an amplifier with a forward gain of $A$. It has a feedback network that returns a fraction of the output, called $\beta$. 
Because we are using positive feedback, the signal from the feedback network adds directly to the input.

The closed-loop transfer function ($A_f$) for positive feedback is:

$$
A_f = \frac{V_o}{V_i} = \frac{A}{1 - A\beta}
$$

### The Condition for Oscillation
An oscillator must generate an output voltage ($V_o \neq 0$) even when the external input voltage is absolutely zero ($V_i = 0$).

Look at the basic equation: $V_o = A_f \times V_i$. 
If $V_i = 0$, the only mathematically possible way for $V_o$ to be non-zero is if the gain $A_f$ approaches infinity ($A_f \to \infty$).

For the closed-loop gain $A_f$ to reach infinity, the denominator of the transfer function must be exactly zero:

$$
1 - A\beta = 0
$$

$$
\implies \mathbf{A\beta = 1}
$$

Because $A$ and $\beta$ are complex numbers, they have both magnitude and phase. The equation $A\beta = 1$ actually contains two strict conditions. We call these two conditions the **Barkhausen Criterion**.

---

## The Two Barkhausen Criteria

### 1. The Amplitude (Gain) Condition
The magnitude of the loop gain must be exactly 1.

$$
\boxed{ |A\beta| = 1 }
$$

- If $|A\beta| < 1$, the oscillations will decay and slowly die out.
- If $|A\beta| > 1$, the oscillations will grow exponentially. They will grow until the amplifier hits its physical power limits and saturates. This clips the sine wave and turns it into a distorted square wave.

### 2. The Phase Condition
The total phase shift around the entire closed loop must be exactly $360^\circ$ (which is the same as $0^\circ$).

$$
\boxed{ \angle A + \angle \beta = 2n\pi } \quad (\text{where } n = 0, 1, 2...)
$$

- The signal travels through the amplifier. The amplifier might shift the phase. Then it travels through the feedback network. The feedback network definitely shifts the phase. When the signal arrives back at the starting input point, it must be perfectly in phase with where it started. This ensures it adds constructively to sustain the loop.

## 2. Frequency and Amplitude Stability
*[Appeared in: 2022 Q8(a), 2017 Q4(c), 2023 Q4(c)]*

Exams often ask follow-up questions about stability.

### Starting Condition vs. Steady State
If $|A\beta| = 1$ is strictly required for a perfect sine wave, how does the oscillator start in the first place?
1. **Starting Phase:** The circuit is deliberately designed so that the initial loop gain is slightly greater than 1 ($|A\beta| > 1$). When you turn the power on, tiny thermal noise triggers the loop. The oscillations grow rapidly.
2. **Steady State Phase:** As the wave amplitude grows very large, the transistors get pushed toward their non-linear saturation regions. This naturally lowers their gain $A$. The amplitude stabilizes precisely at the exact point where the reduced gain makes $|A\beta| = 1$. 

### Frequency Stability
Why do oscillators drift off their designed frequency over time?
As transistors heat up, their internal parasitic capacitances change. This physically shifts the phase of the amplifier ($\angle A$). To maintain the Barkhausen phase condition ($360^\circ$), the feedback network ($\angle \beta$) is forced to shift its phase to compensate. Since $\beta$'s phase is tightly tied to the operating frequency, the oscillation frequency is forced to drift!

**How to fix it:** A stable oscillator must have a feedback network with a very steep phase-versus-frequency curve ($d\phi/d\omega$). If the curve is extremely steep, a massive phase shift only requires a microscopic change in frequency. **Crystal Oscillators** have the steepest phase curves in existence. This makes them incredibly stable.

## ⚡ Exam Tips
- **The proof is short.** The mathematical derivation is only 4 lines long. Just write the positive feedback formula $A / (1 - A\beta)$, set the denominator to exactly zero, and state the $A\beta = 1$ Magnitude and Phase components.
- **Do not mix up feedback types.** Do not confuse the Barkhausen condition ($|A\beta| = 1$) with the instability limit in negative feedback systems ($|A\beta| \ge 1$ at $-180^\circ$). They are the same underlying math. However, in oscillators we want it to happen. In negative feedback amplifiers, we try to prevent it.
