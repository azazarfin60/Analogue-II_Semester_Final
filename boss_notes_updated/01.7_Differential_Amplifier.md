# Differential Amplifier
> **Topic:** Multistage Amplifiers | **Priority:** 🟢 OCCASIONAL | **Exam Frequency:** 2/8 years

## Basic Knowledge: Dealing with Noise

Imagine you send a tiny electrical signal through a long wire. That wire acts like an antenna. It picks up random electrical noise from the air, power lines, and other machines. When the signal reaches the amplifier, the noise gets amplified too. The noise ruins the signal.

How do we solve this? We use two wires instead of one. We send the signal down one wire. We send the exact opposite (inverted) signal down the second wire. 

As these two wires travel together, they pick up the exact same noise from the environment. The noise on wire 1 is identical to the noise on wire 2.

At the receiving end, we use a Differential Amplifier. This amplifier looks at the two wires. It subtracts the voltage of wire 2 from the voltage of wire 1. It only amplifies the difference between them. 

Because the noise is exactly the same on both wires, subtracting them cancels the noise completely. The original signal gets amplified perfectly. This is why the differential amplifier is the most important circuit in modern electronics. It forms the input stage of every operational amplifier.

## The Two Signal Types

A differential amplifier handles two types of signals at the same time:

- **Differential signal ($V_d$):** This is the difference between the two inputs. The amplifier takes this signal and multiplies it by a large gain.
- **Common-mode signal ($V_{cm}$):** This is the average of the two inputs. It represents the noise that is common to both wires. The amplifier tries to block or reject this signal.

We measure how well the amplifier works using the Common-Mode Rejection Ratio (CMRR). CMRR is the ratio of differential gain to common-mode gain. A higher CMRR means the amplifier rejects noise better.

## Symmetrical BJT Differential Pair Circuit

```
             +V_CC                        +V_CC
               |                            |
              [R_C]                        [R_C]
               |                            |
         V_o1 -+                            +- V_o2
               |                            |
          C1  /                              \ C2
   V_i1 --| Q1                           Q2 |-- V_i2
            \                              /
              +--------- E --------+
                         |
                        [R_EE]
                         |
                       -V_EE
```

The two transistors share a common emitter node. This node connects to the negative supply through a tail resistor ($R_{EE}$). In integrated circuits, designers replace this resistor with a constant current source. The circuit is perfectly symmetrical. Both collector resistors match. Both transistors are identical.

## Step-by-Step DC Analysis (Zero-Input)
*[2017 Q3(c)]*

Exams love to ask about the zero-input state. You must find the collector voltages when both inputs sit at 0V.

**Given Values:** $V_{CC} = +9\text{ V}$, $V_{EE} = -9\text{ V}$, $R_{EE} = 3.3\text{ k}\Omega$, $R_C = 3.9\text{ k}\Omega$, $V_{BE} = 0.7\text{ V}$.

**Step 1: Find the shared emitter voltage.** 
Both bases connect to ground (0V). The emitter sits one diode drop below the base.

$$
V_E = V_B - V_{BE} = 0 - 0.7 = -0.7\text{ V}
$$

**Step 2: Find the total tail current.** 
This current flows down through the tail resistor $R_{EE}$. We find the voltage across the resistor and divide by its resistance.

$$
I_{EE} = \frac{V_E - (-V_{EE})}{R_{EE}} = \frac{-0.7 - (-9)}{3.3\text{k}} = \frac{8.3}{3300} \approx 2.515\text{ mA}
$$

**Step 3: Split the current equally.** 
The circuit is perfectly symmetrical. The total tail current splits exactly in half. Each transistor gets half of the current.

$$
I_{E1} = I_{E2} = \frac{I_{EE}}{2} = \frac{2.515}{2} = 1.258\text{ mA}
$$

We assume the collector current roughly equals the emitter current:

$$
I_{C1} = I_{C2} \approx 1.258\text{ mA}
$$

**Step 4: Find the collector voltages.**
We calculate the voltage drop across each collector resistor and subtract it from the top supply voltage.

$$
V_{o1} = V_{CC} - I_{C1} R_C = 9 - (1.258 \times 3.9) = 9 - 4.906 = \boxed{4.094\text{ V}}
$$

$$
V_{o2} = V_{CC} - I_{C2} R_C = 9 - 4.906 = \boxed{4.094\text{ V}}
$$

When the inputs are zero, both outputs are identical. The difference between the outputs is zero. We say the amplifier is balanced.

## Key Concepts and Math

### Gain Equations

If we measure the output from just one collector to ground, we call it a single-ended output. 

The differential gain ($A_d$) for a single-ended output is:

$$
A_d = \frac{-g_m R_C}{2}
$$

The common-mode gain ($A_{cm}$) for a single-ended output is:

$$
A_{cm} = \frac{-R_C}{2R_{EE}}
$$

The Common-Mode Rejection Ratio (CMRR) is the ratio of these two gains:

$$
\text{CMRR} = \frac{|A_d|}{|A_{cm}|} = g_m R_{EE}
$$

Notice that $R_{EE}$ is in the CMRR equation. To get a huge CMRR, you need a huge $R_{EE}$. A constant current source acts like an infinite resistor. That is why IC designers use current mirrors instead of plain resistors for the tail. It provides massive CMRR.

### Signal Math

The differential input voltage is:

$$
V_d = V_{i1} - V_{i2}
$$

The common-mode input voltage is:

$$
V_{cm} = \frac{V_{i1} + V_{i2}}{2}
$$

The total output voltage combines both effects:

$$
V_o = A_d \cdot V_d + A_{cm} \cdot V_{cm}
$$

## ⚡ Exam Tips

1. **Split the tail current equally.** Under zero-input conditions, the tail current splits perfectly in half. Do not forget this simple step. It is the key to solving the DC analysis problem.
2. **Link CMRR to $R_{EE}$.** A bigger tail resistance gives better common-mode rejection. If an exam asks how to improve CMRR, tell them to increase $R_{EE}$ or use a current source.
3. **Know your outputs.** Single-ended means you measure from one collector to ground. Differential means you measure the voltage difference between the two collectors ($V_{o1} - V_{o2}$).
