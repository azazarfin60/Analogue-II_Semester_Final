# Log and Antilog Amplifiers
> **Topic:** Op-Amp Applications | **Priority:** 🟢 OCCASIONAL | **Exam Frequency:** 2/8 years

## Basic Knowledge: The Dynamic Range Problem

In signal processing, we often deal with signals that have a massive dynamic range. For example, a radio receiver might need to process a microscopic 1 $\mu$V signal one second, and a massive 1 V signal the next. 

A standard linear amplifier cannot handle this. If you set the gain high enough to read the tiny signal, the large signal will completely saturate the amplifier and crash it. If you set the gain low enough to handle the large signal, the tiny signal will disappear into the background noise.

We need a way to compress this massive dynamic range into a manageable voltage window.

## Why Use Logarithmic Amplifiers?

A Logarithmic Amplifier solves the dynamic range problem through mathematical compression. It provides huge gain for tiny signals. It provides very small gain for massive signals. This keeps everything within a usable voltage range.

How do we build one? We exploit a quirk of semiconductor physics. The PN junction inside a diode or a Bipolar Junction Transistor (BJT) has an inherently logarithmic relationship between its voltage and its current. We can harness this physics to do our math for us.

## 1. The Logarithmic Amplifier Derivation
*[Appeared in: 2022 Q7(c), 2020 Q7(b)]*

The classic log amp places a BJT inside the feedback loop of an inverting amplifier.

### The Circuit Diagram

```
                     Q1 (BJT)
                   +--|<|---+ (Collector to Emitter)
                   |  C   E |
        Vin o-[ R ]+--o (-) |
                      |     |
        Gnd o--------(+)----+---o Vout
```
- The input $V_{in}$ goes through resistor $R$ to the inverting terminal.
- The BJT has its Base grounded. Its Collector connects to the inverting terminal. Its Emitter connects to the output.

### The Step-by-Step Derivation

**Step 1: Apply Virtual Ground**
Because the non-inverting terminal connects to ground, the op-amp forces the inverting terminal to become a virtual ground ($V_- = 0\text{V}$).

**Step 2: Find the Input Current**
The current flowing from the source through resistor $R$ is simple to find using Ohm's Law:

$$
I_{in} = \frac{V_{in} - 0}{R} = \frac{V_{in}}{R}
$$

**Step 3: Track the Current**
An ideal op-amp draws zero input current. Because of this, all of $I_{in}$ must flow up and straight into the Collector of the BJT.

$$
I_C = I_{in} = \frac{V_{in}}{R}
$$

**Step 4: The BJT Current Equation**
We know from semiconductor physics that the collector current of a BJT is exponentially related to its Base-Emitter voltage ($V_{BE}$):

$$
I_C = I_s e^{V_{BE} / V_T}
$$

*(Where $I_s$ is the reverse saturation current, and $V_T$ is the thermal voltage, which is about $26\text{ mV}$ at room temperature).*

**Step 5: Define $V_{BE}$ in terms of the circuit**
The base of the BJT ties to the virtual ground ($V_B = 0$). The emitter ties directly to the output ($V_E = V_{out}$).
Therefore:

$$
V_{BE} = V_B - V_E = 0 - V_{out} = -V_{out}
$$

**Step 6: Substitute and Solve**
Plug $-V_{out}$ into the exponent. Substitute $I_C$ with $V_{in}/R$:

$$
\frac{V_{in}}{R} = I_s e^{-V_{out} / V_T}
$$

Divide both sides by $I_s$:

$$
\frac{V_{in}}{I_s R} = e^{-V_{out} / V_T}
$$

Take the natural logarithm ($\ln$) of both sides. This cancels out the exponential function:

$$
\ln \left( \frac{V_{in}}{I_s R} \right) = -\frac{V_{out}}{V_T}
$$

Rearrange to solve for the final output voltage $V_{out}$:

$$
\boxed{ V_{out} = -V_T \ln \left( \frac{V_{in}}{I_s R} \right) }
$$

**Result:** The output voltage is proportional to the natural logarithm of the input voltage!

## 2. The Antilogarithmic Amplifier

If we swap the positions of the BJT and the resistor, we reverse the mathematical operation. This creates an Antilog (Exponential) amplifier.

### The Circuit Architecture
- The BJT is now placed at the input side. The Emitter connects to the source. The Base is grounded. The Collector connects to the inverting terminal.
- The resistor $R$ is now placed in the feedback loop.

### The Math
1. The input voltage applies directly across the Base-Emitter junction: $V_{BE} = V_{in}$.
2. This generates a massive exponential collector current inside the BJT: $I_C = I_s e^{V_{in} / V_T}$.
3. This entire current is forced to flow through the feedback resistor $R$.
4. The output voltage is just the voltage drop across $R$:

$$
V_{out} = -I_C R
$$

Substitute the exponential current equation in:

$$
\boxed{ V_{out} = -R I_s e^{V_{in} / V_T} }
$$

**Result:** The output voltage is exponentially proportional to the input voltage.

## ⚡ Exam Tips
- **Watch the BJT orientation.** For the log amp, you must draw the BJT in the correct orientation. The Collector must connect to the virtual ground (inverting pin). The Emitter must connect to the output. If you draw it backwards, the whole derivation fails.
- **Do not drop the negative sign.** Do not forget the negative sign on $V_{out}$ when substituting $V_{BE} = -V_{out}$. This is the most common mathematical mistake students make.
- **Define your variables.** Always state that $V_T$ is the thermal voltage ($\approx 26\text{mV}$ at room temperature).
