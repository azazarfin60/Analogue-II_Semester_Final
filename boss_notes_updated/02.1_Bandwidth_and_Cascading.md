# Bandwidth and Cascading Effect
> **Topic:** Frequency Response | **Priority:** 🟡 IMPORTANT | **Exam Frequency:** 4/8 years

## Basic Knowledge: What is Bandwidth?

An amplifier does not amplify all signal frequencies equally. It works best in the middle frequency range. We call this the mid-band. 

If the input signal frequency drops too low, the amplifier's gain goes down. The point where the gain drops significantly is the lower cutoff frequency ($f_L$). 

If the input signal frequency goes too high, the gain also drops. The point where the gain drops significantly here is the upper cutoff frequency ($f_H$).

The range of frequencies between the lower cutoff and the upper cutoff is the bandwidth. The amplifier works well only inside this bandwidth. 

$$
\text{Bandwidth (BW)} = f_H - f_L
$$

## Why Cascading Shrinks Bandwidth

We often cascade amplifier stages to get a massive voltage gain. We connect the output of stage 1 into the input of stage 2. This multiplies their gains. But there is a hidden cost. Every stage you add shrinks the total bandwidth.

Why does this happen?

1. **At Low Frequencies:** We use coupling and bypass capacitors in the circuit. These capacitors act like high-pass filters. When we cascade stages, we chain multiple high-pass filters together. The combined filter blocks low frequencies much more aggressively. Because of this, the overall lower cutoff frequency ($f_L$) shifts higher.
2. **At High Frequencies:** Every transistor has tiny internal junction capacitances. These act like low-pass filters. When we chain stages, we chain these low-pass filters together. The combined filter blocks high frequencies more aggressively. Because of this, the overall upper cutoff frequency ($f_H$) shifts lower.

Since the lower limit moves up and the upper limit moves down, the bandwidth gets squeezed from both sides. It becomes much narrower.

## The Mathematical Derivation
*[Appeared in: 2023 Q3(b)]*

Exams often ask you to prove exactly how much the upper cutoff frequency shrinks when we cascade $n$ identical amplifier stages. 

### Step 1: Single Stage High-Frequency Response
For a single stage, the voltage gain drops at high frequencies. We write the relative gain equation as:

$$
\frac{A_v(f)}{A_{mid}} = \frac{1}{1 + j(f/f_H)}
$$

Here, $A_{mid}$ is the mid-band gain. The term $f_H$ is the upper cutoff frequency for that single stage.

### Step 2: Cascaded Response
We assume we cascade $n$ identical stages. Their total gain is the product of their individual gains. We write the magnitude of the new relative gain as:

$$
\left| \frac{A_{v(overall)}}{A_{mid(overall)}} \right| = \left( \frac{1}{\sqrt{1 + (f/f_H)^2}} \right)^n
$$

### Step 3: Finding the Overall Cutoff Frequency
We define the overall upper cutoff frequency ($f_{H(overall)}$) as the exact point where the overall relative gain magnitude drops to $1/\sqrt{2}$ (this is the same as -3dB or 0.707). Let's plug $1/\sqrt{2}$ in for the gain and $f_{H(overall)}$ for the frequency:

$$
\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}} = \left( \frac{1}{\sqrt{1 + (f_{H(overall)}/f_H)^2}} \right)^n
$$

Square both sides to remove the square roots:

$$
\frac{1}{2} = \left( \frac{1}{1 + (f_{H(overall)}/f_H)^2} \right)^n
$$

Invert both sides:

$$
2 = \left[ 1 + \left( \frac{f_{H(overall)}}{f_H} \right)^2 \right]^n
$$

Take the $n$-th root of both sides:

$$
2^{1/n} = 1 + \left( \frac{f_{H(overall)}}{f_H} \right)^2
$$

Isolate the frequency ratio on one side:

$$
\left( \frac{f_{H(overall)}}{f_H} \right)^2 = 2^{1/n} - 1
$$

Finally, take the square root to get the final formula:

$$
\boxed{ f_{H(overall)} = f_H \sqrt{2^{1/n} - 1} }
$$

### The Lower Cutoff Formula
We can follow the exact same math process for the low-frequency limit. This gives us the formula for the overall lower cutoff frequency:

$$
\boxed{ f_{L(overall)} = \frac{f_L}{\sqrt{2^{1/n} - 1}} }
$$

---

## What Does This Mean in Practice?

Let's test this formula. We will plug in $n=2$ for a simple two-stage amplifier. We will look at the upper limit:

$$
f_{H(overall)} = f_H \sqrt{2^{1/2} - 1} = f_H \sqrt{\sqrt{2} - 1} = f_H \sqrt{1.414 - 1} = f_H \sqrt{0.414} \approx 0.643 f_H
$$

Just by adding a second identical stage, the upper cutoff frequency drops to about 64% of its original value. The more stages you add, the worse the drop becomes. This math proves that we must trade away bandwidth to gain more voltage amplification.

## ⚡ Exam Tips

- **Explaining the effect.** If an exam asks you to explain the effect, talk about the high-pass and low-pass filters. Explain that they squeeze the limits inward.
- **The mathematical proof.** If the exam asks you to derive it, follow the five steps exactly. It is a standard proof. It guarantees easy marks if you know the steps.
- **The shrinkage factor.** The mathematical term $\sqrt{2^{1/n} - 1}$ is often called the shrinkage factor.
