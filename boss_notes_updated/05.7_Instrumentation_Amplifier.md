# Instrumentation Amplifier
> **Topic:** Op-Amp Applications | **Priority:** 🟢 OCCASIONAL | **Exam Frequency:** 1/8 years

## Basic Knowledge: Reading Weak Sensors

In industrial environments, we often need to read data from physical sensors. We use thermocouples to measure heat. We use strain gauges to measure pressure. 

These sensors produce extremely weak electrical signals, often just a few millivolts. Furthermore, these sensors are very delicate. If your amplifier draws any current from the sensor, the voltage drops. The reading becomes completely inaccurate.

To make things worse, factories are filled with heavy machinery. This machinery generates massive electromagnetic noise. This 50Hz noise hits the long sensor wires and adds 10V of noise on top of your tiny 10mV signal.

We need a special amplifier that can read the signal without drawing any current. We also need it to perfectly reject all the heavy noise. 

---

## Why Do We Need It?
*[Appeared in: 2020 Q2(c)]*

Standard differential amplifiers are good, but they have two major flaws when dealing with these tiny industrial sensors:
1. **Low Input Impedance:** A standard differential amplifier uses input resistors. These resistors draw physical current from the weak sensor. This causes voltage drops and ruins the measurement.
2. **Fixed CMRR:** We need massive Common-Mode Rejection Ratio (CMRR) to isolate the tiny signal from the huge factory noise. Standard differential amplifiers max out around 90 dB because physical resistors are never perfectly matched.

The **Instrumentation Amplifier** solves both problems. It adds two "Buffer" op-amps to the front of a standard differential amplifier. This creates a powerful 3-Op-Amp circuit.

---

## The 3-Op-Amp Architecture

```
        V1 o----(+)---\           R
                      [ A1  ]-o--[ ]---\
        Gnd o---[Rg]--/                |
                 |                     |----(+)---\
                 +-----------------\   |          [ A3  ]---o Vout
                 |                 |   |----(-)--/
        Gnd o---[Rg]--\                |
                      [ A2  ]-o--[ ]---/
        V2 o----(+)---/           R
```
*(Note: A true instrumentation amplifier usually has a single resistor $R_g$ bridging the two inverting terminals of A1 and A2, rather than two separate resistors going to ground. Let's analyze the true standard version).*

### The True Standard Configuration
- $A_1$ and $A_2$ are the input buffer stages. The sensor lines $V_1$ and $V_2$ go directly into their non-inverting (+) terminals.
- A single resistor, **$R_{gain}$**, bridges the inverting (-) terminals of $A_1$ and $A_2$.
- Feedback resistors ($R_1$) connect the outputs of $A_1$ and $A_2$ back to their own inverting terminals.
- $A_3$ is a standard differential amplifier stage with its own resistors $R_2$ and $R_3$.

### Stage 1: The Input Buffers ($A_1$ & $A_2$)
Because the input wires go straight into the non-inverting terminals, the input impedance is essentially infinite. The amplifier draws zero current. The delicate sensor is not loaded at all.

Furthermore, because $R_{gain}$ bridges the two op-amps, it creates a unique differential gain stage.
By the virtual short principle, the voltage at the top of $R_{gain}$ is $V_1$. The voltage at the bottom is $V_2$. 
The current through $R_{gain}$ is exactly $(V_1 - V_2) / R_{gain}$.
This exact same current must flow through both feedback resistors $R_1$.

This cleverly amplifies the *difference* between the signals. However, it simply passes any *common-mode* noise straight through with a gain of exactly 1!
The differential gain of the first stage is:

$$
A_{diff1} = 1 + \frac{2 R_1}{R_{gain}}
$$

### Stage 2: The Differential Amplifier ($A_3$)
The outputs of the buffer stage ($V_{o1}$ and $V_{o2}$) feed directly into $A_3$. This is a standard differential amplifier.
This stage subtracts the two signals. This completely eliminates the common-mode noise that the first stage passed through.
The gain of this second stage is simply:

$$
A_{diff2} = \frac{R_3}{R_2}
$$

### The Total Output Equation

$$
V_{out} = \left( \frac{R_3}{R_2} \right) \left( 1 + \frac{2 R_1}{R_{gain}} \right) (V_2 - V_1)
$$

---

## The Key Advantages

1. **Infinite Input Impedance:** No current is drawn from the sensor. Measurements stay perfectly accurate.
2. **Astronomical CMRR:** Common-mode noise is not amplified by the first stage, but the true differential signal is multiplied massively. This makes the resulting CMRR vastly superior to a standard op-amp.
3. **Single-Resistor Tuning:** To change the total gain of the entire amplifier, you only need to change *one* physical resistor ($R_{gain}$). In a standard differential amplifier, you have to change two matched resistors perfectly simultaneously. If you fail to match them, you ruin the CMRR.

## ⚡ Exam Tips
- **Drawing the circuit.** If asked to draw it, remember the "H" shape at the front. Draw two op-amps. Put the inputs on the (+) pins. Feed the outputs back to the (-) pins. Then draw a single resistor $R_{gain}$ bridging the two (-) terminals together.
- **The keyword.** The phrase "Single-Resistor Tuning" is a massive keyword. It is the primary reason why instrumentation amplifiers are sold as pre-packaged commercial ICs.
