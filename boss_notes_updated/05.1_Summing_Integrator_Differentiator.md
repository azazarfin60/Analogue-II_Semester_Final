# Summing, Integrator, and Differentiator
> **Topic:** Op-Amp Applications | **Priority:** 🔴 CRITICAL | **Exam Frequency:** 7/8 years

## Basic Knowledge: Analog Computers

Before digital computers existed, engineers used analog computers to solve complex math problems. An operational amplifier got its name because it performs mathematical "operations" on voltages. 

By arranging resistors and capacitors around an op-amp, you can build physical circuits that do real math. You can add numbers together. You can multiply a signal by a constant. You can even do calculus. You can build a circuit that finds the derivative of a signal over time. You can build another circuit that finds the integral of a signal over time.

These mathematical circuits form the foundation for control systems, audio synthesizers, and signal processing.

---

## 1. The Inverting Summing Amplifier

The summing amplifier is the backbone of analog math. It allows us to add multiple voltage signals together. Best of all, we can give each input signal its own independent scaling factor (gain).

### The Circuit Diagram
```
        V1 o---[ R1 ]---+
                        |      Rf
        V2 o---[ R2 ]---+---[    ]---+
                        |            |
        V3 o---[ R3 ]---+--(-)       |
                           [ Av ]----+---o Vout
        Gnd o--------------(+)
```

### The Math

Because the non-inverting terminal connects to ground, the inverting terminal becomes a **Virtual Ground** (0V). 

This is the secret to the summing amplifier. Because all input resistors meet at 0V, the current from each input branch is completely independent of the others. They do not interfere with each other.

We find the current for each branch using simple Ohm's law:

$$
I_1 = \frac{V_1}{R_1}, \quad I_2 = \frac{V_2}{R_2}, \quad I_3 = \frac{V_3}{R_3}
$$

By Kirchhoff's Current Law (KCL), all these individual currents must sum together at the central node. The total current then flows through the feedback resistor $R_f$:

$$
I_{total} = I_1 + I_2 + I_3
$$

The output voltage is simply the voltage drop across $R_f$. It is negative because the current flows away from the virtual ground toward the output:

$$
V_{out} = -I_{total} R_f = - \left( \frac{V_1}{R_1} + \frac{V_2}{R_2} + \frac{V_3}{R_3} \right) R_f
$$

We distribute $R_f$ to get the final formula:

$$
\boxed{ V_{out} = - \left( \frac{R_f}{R_1}V_1 + \frac{R_f}{R_2}V_2 + \frac{R_f}{R_3}V_3 \right) }
$$

## 2. Practical Integrators and Differentiators
*[Appeared in: 2024 Q5(b), 2023 Q5(b)]*

In theory, making an integrator or differentiator is very simple. You just replace either the input resistor $R_1$ or the feedback resistor $R_f$ with a capacitor. 
- **Ideal Integrator:** Uses an input resistor ($R$) and a feedback capacitor ($C$).
- **Ideal Differentiator:** Uses an input capacitor ($C$) and a feedback resistor ($R$).

But ideal circuits fail in the real world. Exams frequently ask *why* they fail and *how* to fix them.

### The Practical Integrator Problem and Solution

**The Problem:** The feedback element is a capacitor. At DC (0 Hz), a capacitor acts like an open circuit. It has infinite resistance. Because of this, the DC gain of the op-amp becomes $A_v = -R_f/R_{in} = -\infty/R = -\infty$. 

Even a microscopic 1 mV DC offset voltage at the input will be integrated infinitely over time. The output voltage will slowly ramp up until it hits the power supply limit ($V_{sat}$). The amplifier crashes and becomes useless.

**The Solution:** Connect a large physical resistor $R_f$ in parallel with the feedback capacitor $C$. 
This provides a safe path for the DC current. It caps the maximum low-frequency gain at a safe, finite value ($-R_f/R_{in}$). This prevents the op-amp from saturating.

### The Practical Differentiator Problem and Solution

**The Problem:** The gain of an ideal differentiator increases linearly with frequency ($|A_v| = \omega RC$). At very high frequencies, the gain becomes massive. 

This means the circuit will aggressively amplify any high-frequency electrical noise (like radio static) present in the room. This noise will completely drown out your actual low-frequency signal. Also, this massive high-frequency amplification often pushes the op-amp into instability and oscillation.

**The Solution:** Add a small resistor $R_i$ in series with the input capacitor. Then add a small capacitor $C_f$ in parallel with the feedback resistor. 
This turns the circuit into a low-pass filter at very high frequencies. It rolls off the gain before the noise can be amplified to dangerous levels.
