# CMRR and Slew Rate
> **Topic:** Op-Amp Fundamentals | **Priority:** 🔴 CRITICAL | **Exam Frequency:** 6/8 years

## Basic Knowledge: Dealing with Noise

An operational amplifier has two input pins. It is designed to amplify the exact difference between them. 

But what happens if a signal hits both input pins at the exact same time, with the exact same voltage? We call this a common-mode signal ($V_{cm}$). 

In real circuits, this common-mode signal is usually noise. Long wires act like antennas. They pick up 50Hz or 60Hz electromagnetic hum from power lines and lights. This noise enters both wires equally.

An ideal op-amp would subtract them perfectly. The noise on pin 1 minus the noise on pin 2 equals zero. The ideal op-amp outputs nothing. 

However, real op-amps are not perfectly balanced inside. A tiny bit of that noise leaks through to the output. We must measure how well the op-amp rejects this noise.

---

## 1. Common-Mode Rejection Ratio (CMRR)

**Definition:** CMRR measures the ratio between two things. It compares how well the op-amp amplifies the desired differential signal ($A_d$) against how poorly it amplifies the common-mode noise signal ($A_{cm}$).

$$
\text{CMRR} = \frac{|A_d|}{|A_{cm}|}
$$

Engineers usually express CMRR in decibels (dB):

$$
\text{CMRR (dB)} = 20\log_{10} \left( \frac{|A_d|}{|A_{cm}|} \right)
$$

A higher CMRR means much better noise rejection. A standard LM741 op-amp has a CMRR of about 90 dB. This means it amplifies the true difference 31,622 times more than it amplifies the noise.

---

## Basic Knowledge: The Speed Limit

Imagine you input a perfect, sharp square wave into an op-amp. The input jumps from 0V to 5V instantly. In an ideal world, the output would also jump instantly.

In the real world, the op-amp has tiny parasitic capacitors inside its transistor stages. You cannot change the voltage across a capacitor instantly. It takes time for the current to charge it up. This creates a hard physical speed limit for the amplifier.

---

## 2. Slew Rate (SR)
*[Appeared in: 2019 Q4(b), 2017 Q4(a), 2017 Q6(b)]*

**Definition:** Slew Rate is the absolute maximum physical rate of change of the output voltage. It defines exactly how fast the output can move from one voltage level to another.

$$
SR = \left. \frac{dv_o}{dt} \right|_{max}
$$

We usually measure it in Volts per microsecond ($\text{V}/\mu\text{s}$). The classic 741 op-amp has a slew rate of $0.5\text{ V}/\mu\text{s}$. 

### The Triangle Wave Distortion Problem
Imagine you input a high-frequency sine wave with a large amplitude. The steep sides of the sine wave force the output voltage to rise very fast. 

If the sine wave asks the output to rise faster than the Slew Rate limit, the op-amp fails. It cannot push enough current to keep up. The output falls behind. It turns the smooth sine wave into a straight-line ramp. 

**Result:** Slew rate limiting distorts high-frequency, large-amplitude sine waves into **triangular waves**.

---

## 3. Derivation: Maximum Frequency ($f_{max}$)

How fast can a sine wave be before it hits the slew rate limit? Let's derive the standard formula.

**Step 1:** Assume the output is a perfect, undistorted sine wave with a peak amplitude of $V_p$:

$$
v_o(t) = V_p \sin(2\pi f t)
$$

**Step 2:** Find the required rate of change. Take the mathematical derivative with respect to time:

$$
\frac{dv_o}{dt} = \frac{d}{dt} [V_p \sin(2\pi f t)] = 2\pi f V_p \cos(2\pi f t)
$$

**Step 3:** The maximum rate of change happens when the cosine term equals 1. This occurs exactly as the wave crosses the zero line:

$$
\left. \frac{dv_o}{dt} \right|_{max} = 2\pi f V_p
$$

**Step 4:** To prevent any distortion, this required rate of change must be less than or equal to the op-amp's physical Slew Rate limit ($SR$):

$$
SR \ge 2\pi f V_p
$$

**Step 5:** Isolate the frequency variable to find the maximum allowed limit:

$$
\boxed{ f_{max} = \frac{SR}{2\pi V_p} }
$$

---

## Worked Numerical 1: Find $f_{max}$
*[Appeared in: 2017 Q6(b)]*

**Given Values:** 
- Inverting op-amp with $R_f = 100\text{ k}\Omega$ and $R_1 = 10\text{ k}\Omega$. 
- Slew rate $SR = 0.5\text{ V}/\mu\text{s}$. 
- Input signal $v_i(t) = 10\sin(2\pi f t)\text{ mV}$ peak. 

**Goal:** Calculate $f_{max}$ without distortion.

**Step 1: Calculate Closed-Loop Gain ($A_{CL}$)**

$$
A_{CL} = -\frac{R_f}{R_1} = -\frac{100\text{ k}}{10\text{ k}} = -10
$$

**Step 2: Determine Output Peak Voltage ($V_p$)**
The input is 10 mV peak. Multiply this by the gain magnitude to find the output peak voltage:

$$
V_p = |A_{CL}| \times V_{p(in)} = 10 \times 10\text{ mV} = 100\text{ mV} = \mathbf{0.1\text{ V}}
$$

**Step 3: Calculate $f_{max}$**
First, convert the Slew Rate into standard SI units (Volts per second):

$$
SR = 0.5\text{ V}/\mu\text{s} = 0.5 \times 10^6\text{ V/s}
$$

Now apply the formula:

$$
f_{max} = \frac{SR}{2\pi V_p} = \frac{0.5 \times 10^6}{2\pi \times 0.1} = \frac{5 \times 10^6}{2\pi} \approx \boxed{ \mathbf{795.8\text{ kHz}} }
$$

## Worked Numerical 2: Find Maximum Input Voltage
*[Appeared in: 2019 Q4(b)]*

**Given Values:** 
- Closed-loop gain = $10$. 
- Operating frequency $f = 40\text{ kHz}$. 
- Slew rate $SR = 0.5\text{ V}/\mu\text{s}$. 

**Goal:** Find the maximum peak-to-peak input voltage ($V_{p-p(in)}$) before distortion occurs.

**Step 1: Calculate maximum allowed output peak ($V_p$)**
Rearrange the standard formula $SR \ge 2\pi f V_p$:

$$
V_p = \frac{SR}{2\pi f} = \frac{0.5 \times 10^6}{2\pi \times 40,000} = \frac{500,000}{80,000\pi} = \frac{50}{8\pi} \approx \mathbf{1.989\text{ V peak}}
$$

**Step 2: Convert to peak-to-peak output**

$$
V_{p-p(out)} = 2 \times V_p = 2 \times 1.989 = \mathbf{3.978\text{ V}_{p-p}}
$$

**Step 3: Work backwards to find the input**
Divide the output voltage by the amplifier gain to find what input caused it:

$$
V_{p-p(in)} = \frac{V_{p-p(out)}}{\text{Gain}} = \frac{3.978}{10} \approx \boxed{ \mathbf{0.398\text{ V}_{p-p}} } \quad (\text{or } 398\text{ mV}_{p-p})
$$

## ⚡ Exam Tips
- **Unit Conversion is the Biggest Trap.** You MUST convert $\mu\text{s}$ to seconds. $0.5\text{ V}/\mu\text{s}$ is $500,000\text{ V/s}$. If you do not multiply by $10^6$, your frequency answer will be wrong by a million times.
- **Watch the Voltage Type.** The formula requires $V_p$ (peak voltage). It does NOT use RMS voltage. It does NOT use peak-to-peak. If the question gives you peak-to-peak, divide by 2 before you start.
- **Use the Output Voltage.** The formula cares about the **Output** voltage. If the problem gives you the input voltage, you must multiply by the gain first to find the output.
