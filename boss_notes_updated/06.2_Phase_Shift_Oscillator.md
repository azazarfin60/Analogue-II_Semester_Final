# RC Phase-Shift Oscillator
> **Topic:** Oscillators | **Priority:** 🟡 IMPORTANT | **Exam Frequency:** 3/8 years

## Basic Knowledge: Shifting the Phase

To build an oscillator, the Barkhausen criterion says we must feed the output signal back to the input perfectly in phase. The total phase shift around the loop must be 360 degrees (which is the same as 0 degrees).

If we use a standard Inverting Operational Amplifier, it naturally flips the signal upside down. This instantly gives us a -180 degree phase shift. We are halfway there. We just need to shift the signal by another 180 degrees to get back to a full 360 degrees.

How do we do this? We use Resistor-Capacitor (RC) filters. 

When an AC signal passes through a capacitor, the voltage lags behind the current. This physically shifts the phase of the signal. A single RC high-pass filter can theoretically provide up to 90 degrees of phase shift. However, in reality, we can only reliably get about 60 degrees of shift per filter. 

Because of this, we must cascade exactly **three** identical RC filter networks together. Each network provides 60 degrees of shift. Three networks give us the perfect 180 degrees we need.

$$
-180^\circ \text{ (Amplifier)} + 180^\circ \text{ (Feedback)} = 0^\circ \text{ (or } 360^\circ \text{)}
$$

## 1. Frequency and Gain Derivation
*[Appeared in: 2021 Q4(b), 2018 Q5(c)]*

### The Feedback Circuit
The feedback network consists of three series capacitors ($C_1, C_2, C_3$) and three parallel resistors ($R_1, R_2, R_3$) leading to ground. We assume all capacitors are equal ($C$) and all resistors are equal ($R$).

By applying nodal analysis to this 3-stage ladder network, we derive the feedback transfer function $\beta = V_f / V_o$:

$$
\beta(j\omega) = \frac{1}{1 - \frac{5}{\omega^2 R^2 C^2} - j \left( \frac{6}{\omega R C} - \frac{1}{\omega^3 R^3 C^3} \right)}
$$

### Deriving the Resonant Frequency ($f_o$)
According to Barkhausen, the phase shift must be exactly 180 degrees. A 180 degree phase shift means the signal is purely real and negative. There can be absolutely zero imaginary component.
Therefore, we must set the imaginary part of the denominator exactly to zero:

$$
\frac{6}{\omega RC} - \frac{1}{\omega^3 R^3 C^3} = 0
$$

Move the negative term over:

$$
\frac{6}{\omega RC} = \frac{1}{\omega^3 R^3 C^3}
$$

Multiply both sides by $\omega^3 R^3 C^3$:

$$
6 \omega^2 R^2 C^2 = 1
$$

Solve for $\omega$:

$$
\omega^2 = \frac{1}{6 R^2 C^2} \implies \omega = \frac{1}{\sqrt{6} RC}
$$

Since $\omega = 2\pi f_o$, we get the final frequency formula:

$$
\boxed{ f_o = \frac{1}{2\pi \sqrt{6} RC} }
$$

### Deriving the Gain Requirement ($|A| \ge 29$)
Now that we know the frequency where the phase shift is perfect, how much does the RC network weaken the signal? Let's plug $\omega^2 R^2 C^2 = 1/6$ back into the real part of the transfer function.

$$
\beta = \frac{1}{1 - 5 \left( \frac{1}{\omega^2 R^2 C^2} \right)}
$$

$$
\beta = \frac{1}{1 - 5(6)} = \frac{1}{1 - 30} = -\frac{1}{29}
$$

*(The negative sign confirms the 180 degree phase shift is working).*

The Barkhausen amplitude condition states $|A\beta| \ge 1$.

$$
|A \left( -\frac{1}{29} \right)| \ge 1 \implies \boxed{ |A| \ge 29 }
$$

**Conclusion:** The RC network destroys a massive amount of the signal. It reduces the voltage to 1/29th of its original size. To sustain continuous oscillations, the inverting amplifier must be designed with a massive voltage gain of at least 29.

## 2. Worked Design Problem
*[Appeared in: 2019 Q8(c)]*

**Problem Details:**
Design an op-amp based 3-stage RC phase-shift oscillator to operate at a resonant frequency of $f_o = 400\text{ Hz}$.

### Step 1: Select a Practical Capacitor
We must pick an arbitrary, standard capacitor value to start the design.
Let **$C = 0.1\ \mu\text{F}$** ($10^{-7}\text{ F}$).

### Step 2: Calculate the Required Feedback Resistor ($R$)
Use the derived frequency formula:

$$
f_o = \frac{1}{2\pi \sqrt{6} RC}
$$

Rearrange to solve for $R$:

$$
R = \frac{1}{2\pi \sqrt{6} f_o C}
$$

$$
R = \frac{1}{2\pi \times 2.449 \times 400 \times 10^{-7}} = \frac{1}{0.0006155} \approx \mathbf{1624.7\ \Omega}
$$

We will select a standard resistor value of **$1.62\text{ k}\Omega$** for the three feedback network resistors.

### Step 3: Design the Inverting Amplifier
The op-amp is configured as a standard inverting amplifier. It has an input resistor $R_1$ and a feedback resistor $R_F$. 
To prevent loading effects from ruining the delicate RC math, engineers usually choose the input resistor $R_1$ to match the network resistor $R$.

$$
R_1 = 1.62\text{ k}\Omega
$$

We just proved the gain must be at least 29 ($|A| \ge 29$).
For an inverting amplifier, the gain is $|A| = R_F / R_1$.

$$
\frac{R_F}{1.62\text{ k}\Omega} \ge 29 \implies R_F \ge 29 \times 1.62\text{ k}\Omega \approx \mathbf{46.98\text{ k}\Omega}
$$

We will select a standard resistor value of **$47\text{ k}\Omega$** for $R_F$ to guarantee the oscillations start successfully.

## ⚡ Exam Tips
- **Memorize the trick.** The derivation from the raw $\beta$ formula to $f_o$ and $|A|=29$ is heavily tested. Memorize setting the imaginary part to exactly zero to find $\omega$. Then, remember to plug that $\omega$ back into the real part to prove $\beta = 1/29$.
- **Pick normal parts.** In design questions, always start by arbitrarily selecting a realistic capacitor in the nanoFarad or microFarad range ($10\text{nF}$ or $0.1\mu\text{F}$ are safe bets). Never pick 1 Farad.
