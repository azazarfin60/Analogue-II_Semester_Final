# Darlington Pair
> **Topic:** Multistage Amplifiers | **Priority:** 🔴 CRITICAL | **Exam Frequency:** 6/8 years

## Basic Knowledge: The Need for High Current Gain

A standard Bipolar Junction Transistor (BJT) amplifies current. We call this current gain $\beta$. A typical BJT has a $\beta$ between 100 and 200. This is good for many circuits. But some circuits need much more current. For example, motor drivers and power switches need a massive amount of current to run.

If we need a current gain of 10,000, a single BJT is not enough. We solve this problem by using a Darlington Pair.

A Darlington Pair connects two BJTs together to act like one "super transistor." The first transistor (Q1) amplifies the input current. Then, the second transistor (Q2) amplifies that current again. The total current gain becomes the product of both individual gains.

This connection also creates a very high input impedance. This means the circuit will not draw much current from the signal source.

## The Circuit Structure

```
            Collector (C)
            o-----------+
                        |
                        |
                  C1  |/ Q1
        Base (B) o----|
                      |> E1
                        |
                        +----+
                             |
                       C2  |/ Q2
                           |------> Output Emitter (E)
                           |> E2
```

We tie both collectors together. They connect to the same power supply. The emitter of Q1 connects directly to the base of Q2. The input signal enters the base of Q1. The final amplified current exits from the emitter of Q2.

---

## The Step-by-Step Proof: $\beta_D \approx \beta_1 \beta_2$

This mathematical proof appears in almost every exam. The logic is simple and direct. We will prove that the total current gain is the product of the two $\beta$ values.

**Step 1: Start with the input current.**
Let the input current entering the base of Q1 be $I_{B1}$.

**Step 2: Q1 amplifies the base current.**
The collector current of Q1 is:

$$
I_{C1} = \beta_1 I_{B1}
$$

**Step 3: Find Q1's emitter current.**
The emitter current is the sum of the base current and collector current:

$$
I_{E1} = I_{C1} + I_{B1} = (\beta_1 + 1) I_{B1}
$$

**Step 4: The key connection.**
The emitter current of Q1 flows directly into the base of Q2.

$$
I_{B2} = I_{E1} = (\beta_1 + 1) I_{B1}
$$

**Step 5: Q2 amplifies the current again.**
The collector current of Q2 is:

$$
I_{C2} = \beta_2 I_{B2} = \beta_2 (\beta_1 + 1) I_{B1}
$$

**Step 6: Find the total collector current.**
Both collectors tie together. The total collector current is the sum of $I_{C1}$ and $I_{C2}$:

$$
I_C = I_{C1} + I_{C2} = \beta_1 I_{B1} + \beta_2(\beta_1 + 1) I_{B1}
$$

$$
I_C = (\beta_1 + \beta_1\beta_2 + \beta_2) I_{B1}
$$

**Step 7: Calculate the overall current gain.**
We divide the total collector current by the input base current:

$$
\beta_D = \frac{I_C}{I_{B1}} = \beta_1\beta_2 + \beta_1 + \beta_2
$$

**Step 8: Make the practical approximation.**
The values of $\beta_1$ and $\beta_2$ are usually between 50 and 200. Their product ($\beta_1\beta_2$) is much larger than their sum ($\beta_1 + \beta_2$). We can ignore the sum.

$$
\boxed{\beta_D \approx \beta_1 \beta_2}
$$

If $\beta_1 = 100$ and $\beta_2 = 100$, the total gain is about 10,000. Two transistors give a massive current boost.

## Key Features of the Darlington Pair

| Feature | Single BJT | Darlington Pair |
|---|---|---|
| **Current Gain** | $\beta$ (100-200) | $\beta_D \approx \beta_1\beta_2$ (10,000+) |
| **Input Impedance** | $\beta r_e$ | $\beta_1 \beta_2 R_E$ (very high) |
| **Voltage Gain** | $-R_C/r_e$ (high) | $\approx 1$ (emitter follower mode) |
| **$V_{BE}$ Drop** | 0.7 V | **1.4 V** (two junctions in series) |
| **Saturation $V_{CE}$** | ~0.2 V | ~0.9 V (higher than single) |

> **Critical Fact:** The Darlington has two base-emitter junctions in series. The total $V_{BE}$ drop is $V_{BE1} + V_{BE2} \approx 1.4\text{ V}$. It is not 0.7 V.

## Worked Example: Darlington Emitter Follower

*[2019 Q2(b)]*
![[Pasted image 20260607014939.png]]
**Given values:** $R_B = 2.4\text{ M}\Omega$, $R_E = 510\;\Omega$, $\beta_1 = 50$, $\beta_2 = 120$, $V_{CC} = +16\text{ V}$.

### Step 1: DC Bias Analysis

First, find the total current gain:

$$
\beta_D \approx \beta_1 \beta_2 = 50 \times 120 = 6000
$$

Apply KVL around the base-emitter loop. Remember to use two $V_{BE}$ voltage drops:

$$
V_{CC} - I_{B1} R_B - V_{BE1} - V_{BE2} - I_E R_E = 0
$$

We know $I_E = (\beta_D + 1) I_{B1} \approx 6000 \, I_{B1}$. We substitute this into the equation:

$$
16 - I_{B1}(2.4\text{M}) - 0.7 - 0.7 - 6000 \, I_{B1}(510) = 0
$$

$$
14.6 = I_{B1}(2.4\text{M} + 3.06\text{M}) = I_{B1}(5.46\text{M})
$$

$$
I_{B1} = \frac{14.6}{5.46\text{M}} \approx 2.67\;\mu\text{A}
$$

Now calculate the total emitter current:

$$
I_E = 6000 \times 2.67\;\mu\text{A} \approx 16.02\text{ mA}
$$

### Step 2: Dynamic Resistances

Calculate the internal resistance of Q2:

$$
r_{e2} = \frac{26\text{ mV}}{16.02\text{ mA}} \approx 1.62\;\Omega
$$

Find the emitter current of Q1. This is the same as the base current of Q2:

$$
I_{E1} \approx I_{B2} = \frac{I_E}{\beta_2} = \frac{16.02\text{ mA}}{120} \approx 133.5\;\mu\text{A}
$$

Calculate the internal resistance of Q1:

$$
r_{e1} = \frac{26\text{ mV}}{133.5\;\mu\text{A}} \approx 194.7\;\Omega
$$

### Step 3: AC Parameters

**Input impedance at the Darlington base:**

$$
Z_{base} \approx \beta_1[\,r_{e1} + \beta_2(r_{e2} + R_E)\,] \approx \beta_1 \beta_2 R_E = 6000 \times 510 \approx 3.06\text{ M}\Omega
$$

**Total input impedance:**

$$
Z_i = R_B \| Z_{base} = 2.4\text{M} \| 3.06\text{M} \approx 1.34\text{ M}\Omega
$$

### Step 4: Gain Calculation

This circuit is an emitter follower. The voltage gain should be close to 1:

$$
A_v = \frac{R_E}{R_E + r_{e2} + r_{e1}/\beta_2} = \frac{510}{510 + 1.62 + 194.7/120} = \frac{510}{513.24} \approx \boxed{0.994}
$$

The result is nearly 1. This matches our expectations.

Calculate the current gain:

$$
A_i = A_v \times \frac{Z_i}{R_E} = 0.994 \times \frac{1.34\text{M}}{510} \approx \boxed{2612}
$$

The circuit gives massive current gain and a voltage gain of 1. This is the main job of a Darlington pair.

## Worked Example: Voltage-Divider Biased Darlington

*[2024 Q2(b)]*

**Given values:** $V_{CC} = +27\text{ V}$, $R_{B1} = 470\text{k}$, $R_{B2} = 220\text{k}$, $R_C = 1.2\text{k}$, $R_E = 680\;\Omega$ (bypassed), $\beta_1 = \beta_2 = 110$.

$$
\beta_D = 110 \times 110 = 12{,}100
$$

**Thevenin values at the base:**

$$
V_{th} = 27 \times \frac{220\text{k}}{470\text{k} + 220\text{k}} = 27 \times 0.319 \approx 8.61\text{ V}
$$

$$
R_{th} = 470\text{k} \| 220\text{k} = \frac{470 \times 220}{470+220} \approx 149.9\text{ k}\Omega
$$

**KVL Equation:**

$$
8.61 - I_{B1}(149.9\text{k}) - 1.4 - 12100 \, I_{B1}(680) = 0
$$

$$
7.21 = I_{B1}(149.9\text{k} + 8.228\text{M}) = I_{B1}(8.378\text{M})
$$

$$
I_{B1} \approx 0.86\;\mu\text{A}, \quad I_E \approx 10.4\text{ mA}
$$

$$
r_e = \frac{26}{10.4} \approx 2.5\;\Omega
$$

**AC Analysis (with bypassed $R_E$):**

$$
Z_{i2\_base} = \beta_1[\,r_{e1} + \beta_2 \cdot r_e\,] \approx \beta_D \cdot r_e = 12100 \times 2.5 = 30.25\text{ k}\Omega
$$

$$
Z_i = R_{th} \| Z_{i2\_base} = 149.9\text{k} \| 30.25\text{k} \approx 25.2\text{ k}\Omega
$$

$$
A_v = -\frac{R_C}{r_e} = -\frac{1200}{2.5} = -480
$$

$$
A_i = A_v \times \frac{Z_i}{R_C} = -480 \times \frac{25.2\text{k}}{1.2\text{k}} \approx -10{,}080
$$

## ⚡ Exam Tips and Common Mistakes

1. **Remember the two $V_{BE}$ drops.** A common mistake is writing $V_{BE} = 0.7\text{ V}$. It is **1.4 V**. If you miss this, your entire DC calculation will be wrong.
2. **Memorize the proof.** The 7-line $\beta_D$ proof is easy. It gives you 3 or 4 marks in almost every exam.
3. **Know the modes.** If $R_E$ is not bypassed, the circuit acts as an emitter follower. It has huge current gain but voltage gain near 1. If $R_E$ is bypassed, it acts like a CE amplifier. It will have very high voltage gain.
4. **Learn the difference.** Do not confuse the Feedback Pair with the Darlington Pair. Darlington uses two transistors of the same type. The Feedback Pair uses different types (NPN and PNP).
